**Abstract**

Authenticity is highly valued in the marketplace, yet consumers often disagree on
what is authentic and even attribute authenticity to “fake” marketplace entities.
Although prior research has advanced understanding by identifying multiple
types and components of authenticity, it remains loosely connected to broader
psychological theory, limiting integration and predictive power. We propose
a conceptual framework that shifts the focus from _what_ authenticity is to _how_
authenticity judgments are formed. Integrating a systematic review of authenticity
research with dual-­process theories, we position authenticity as the outcome of a
_judgment process_ that proceeds through two routes: a fact-­based route grounded
in deliberate, analytical evaluation of claims and a feeling-­based route based
on intuitive processing, emotional resonance, and automatic associations. This
distinction explains paradoxes in authenticity research and generates novel
predictions regarding (1) the authenticity cues consumers prioritize under each
route and resulting authenticity judgments, (2) the situational and motivational
conditions that activate each route, and (3) downstream implications for brands.
By embedding authenticity judgments within established psychological theory, we
offer a more unified and predictive foundation for authenticity research.

Few qualities are as universally desired in the marketplace as authenticity. Brands strive to achieve authenticity, influencers seek to be perceived as authentic, and
consumers are increasingly drawn to products and experiences that feel “real.” Consequently, authenticity has
become a defining concern in contemporary marketing
(e.g., Brown et al., 2003; Gilmore & Pine, 2007), shaping
how brands position themselves and consumer demand.
Developments like the rise of artificial intelligence
only heighten the craving for authenticity (Jago, 2019).
Conversely, being perceived as inauthentic can be a
death knell for brands, undermining their credibility and
eliciting consumer outrage (Silver et al., 2021).
At its most basic level, authenticity refers to the quality of being real, true, or genuine (Dutton, 2003; Lehman

These puzzling deviations from authenticity's definition as “real” or “true” have inspired a rich set of
frameworks in consumer behavior research that organizes the many meanings embedded in the construct
(e.g., Akbar & Wymer, 2017; Beverland et al., 2008;
Carroll & Wheaton, 2009; Grayson & Martinec, 2004;
Morhart et al., 2015; Moulard et al., 2021; Napoli
et al., 2014; Nunes et al., 2021; Spiggle et al., 2012;
Wang, 1999). Although this work has deepened our
understanding of authenticity's diverse meanings,
multidimensional and often overlapping frameworks
focused on defining authenticity continue to proliferate (Newman, 2019). As a result, authenticity research
remains somewhat fragmented, with ongoing emphasis on definitions diverting attention from other important questions that hold significant theoretical and
managerial implications. Specifically, while prior work
often attributes variation in authenticity to differences
in the consumption context, it has paid far less attention to the fundamental psychological processes that
shape authenticity judgments. Because underlying
psychological processes operate across all contexts,
understanding their influence may enable more parsimonious predictions about when different authenticity
judgments emerge and their marketing implications.
Thus, we propose that advancing authenticity research
involves shifting attention from _what_ authenticity is to
_how_ authenticity judgments are formed.
To do so, we position authenticity as the outcome of
a _judgment proces_ s. This judgment process arises when
consumers attempt to determine whether an entity is
real, true, or genuine—that is, whether the entity is what
it claims or appears to be. Building on dual-­process theorizing, which recognizes that judgments tend to result
from either more deliberate or more intuitive processing
(for a review, see Samson & Voyer, 2012), we propose
that authenticity judgments likewise emerge through
two distinct processing routes: a _fact-­based route_ and a
_feeling-­based route_ . In the fact-­based route, consumers
analytically compare the target entity with information
they perceive as indicative of the real or true nature of
the entity. In the feeling-­based route, consumers rely on
intuitive, holistic processing—drawing on emotional resonance, sensory impressions, and rapid associations—to
determine the authenticity of the entity. Consequently,
variation in authenticity judgments may stem from the
relative extent to which consumers rely on what they _de-_
_duce_ versus what they _feel_ to be authentic.
Integrating dual-­process theorizing with authenticity
judgments offers a new approach to understanding marketplace authenticity by shifting attention away from the
multifaceted nature of authenticity itself to the consumer
information-­processing mechanisms that generate judgments. By focusing on _how_ consumers judge authenticity, our framework clarifies _when_ authenticity processes
activate and how they guide attention to authenticity
cues. Specifically, we develop novel, testable predictions



regarding (1) the authenticity cues consumers prioritize
under the fact-­based or feeling-­based processing routes
and the resulting characteristics of their authenticity
judgments, (2) the situational and motivational conditions that activate fact-­based or feeling-­based judgments
of authenticity, and (3) the downstream implications for
brands.

Importantly, this integration also situates authenticity
research in consumer behavior within well-­established

psychological theories of information processing and
decision making. The fundamental recognition that
people tend to engage in either more deliberate or more
intuitive processing has synthesized and reconciled conflicting findings across many research areas (Chaiken
& Ledgerwood, 2007), including consumer-­relevant domains such as persuasion (e.g., Petty & Cacioppo, 1986),
decision making (e.g., Kahneman, 2011; Kahneman
& Frederick, 2002), and self-­control (e.g., Strack &
Deutsch, 2004). Yet the complexity found in authenticity judgments of marketplace entities has not been
systematically connected to differences in information processing. In part because of its multifaceted and
context-­dependent nature, authenticity has often been
treated as requiring special theoretical treatment and
thus siloed from broader psychological theory. Our
framework instead repositions authenticity as a judgment process, enabling more theoretical “lumping”
rather than “splitting” (Fiske, 2006) and integrating previously fragmented conceptualizations within a broader
theoretical context.
This article is organized as follows. We first review
existing approaches to conceptualizing authenticity in
consumer research. We then introduce a dual-­process
framework of authenticity judgments, showing how the
many meanings identified in prior authenticity research
can be understood as emerging from two processing
routes: a fact-­based route and a feeling-­based route. We
next develop predictions regarding which cues become
salient under each route and how these cues shape authenticity judgments, including degree of epistemic
validity and social consensus. We also examine key
situational and motivational moderators that may activate reliance on either route when forming authenticity
judgments. Finally, we outline the implications of these
routes for key brand outcomes, including consumer loyalty, reputational backlash, and innovation flexibility.

Authenticity plays a central role in evaluations
across many consumption domains, including art
(Dutton, 2003; Newman & Bloom, 2012), tourism
(Taylor, 2001; Wang, 1999), music (Peterson, 1997),
cuisine (Carroll & Wheaton, 2009; Kovács et al., 2014),
wine and beer (Beverland, 2006; Beverland et al., 2008),
collectables (Newman et al., 2011), entertainment

(Rifkin et al., 2023; Rose & Wood, 2005), beauty

(Valsesia et al., 2016; Valsesia & Diehl, 2022). Perceived
authenticity enhances a variety of marketing outcomes,
including brand trust (e.g., Hernandez-­Fernandez

Despite agreement about its importance, authenticity research in consumer behavior has long struggled to arrive at a shared definition (e.g., Akbar
& Wymer, 2017; Newman & Smith, 2016a; Nunes
et al., 2021; Reisinger & Steiner, 2006; Södergren, 2021).
Although there is consensus that authenticity, in its
simplest terms, refers to being real, true, or genuine
(e.g., Dutton, 2003; Lehman et al., 2019), this definition
does not capture the full range of consumer responses.
This limitation arises in part because consumers apply
authenticity to many entities—including brands, products, experiences, and people—and across diverse contexts, contributing to conceptual diffusion. Describing
a Louis Vuitton bag, Mexican cuisine, a dance performance, or a social media influencer as authentic, for
instance, can evoke different associations and meanings. Accordingly, authenticity has been characterized
as a “dimension word,” whose meaning becomes clear
only when the relevant dimension or target is specified
(Dutton, 2003).
To address this variation in consumers' ascriptions
of authenticity, researchers have organized its various
meanings into conceptual frameworks. Below, we review key authenticity frameworks in consumer behavior and in consumer-­relevant contexts (see Table 1 for

**The typology approach to authenticity**


One common approach to conceptualizing authenticity
divides it into distinct types. This typology approach
emerged across consumer contexts where researchers
observed that consumer perceptions of what constitutes
authenticity extend beyond traditional interpretations of
“real” or “true.” Typology frameworks therefore aim to
organize this variation.
Early work on consumer perceptions of authenticity
in the tourism context assumed the “museum-­linkage”
definition of authenticity (Wang, 1999), which is concerned with whether an object originates from its purported source or provenance—that is, whether it is an
“original” (Trilling, 1972). Under this view, authenticity involves verifying a historical claim (e.g., whether
a heritage site, cultural performance, or local tradition reflects its claimed place and time). However, this
definition did not capture cases in which consumers
perceive authenticity even when historical accuracy
is absent, or explain why experiential and emotional



aspects of tourism often outweigh strict historical
verification.

Addressing these limitations, Wang (1999) proposed
three types of authenticity. _Objective authenticity_ aligns
with the museum-­linked view and focuses on verifying the factual origin of an object or cultural artifact.
_Constructed authenticity_ recognizes that consumers
may perceive something as authentic not because it is
historically accurate, but because it accords with their
preexisting schemas or expectations. For instance, a
cultural dance might be seen as authentic even if it is
not historically accurate because it fits a consumer's
schemas of that culture formed through their own experiences over time. _Existential authenticity_, by contrast, shifts attention from objects to experiences and
emphasizes a consumer's internal state of being as the
source of authenticity. Here, authenticity concerns feeling “true to oneself,” rather than verifying attributes
of an external entity. For example, a consumer may
travel to a new culture to reconnect with their authentic self, temporarily liberated from the social expectations that constrain their everyday life. Thus, although
cultural objects may inform authenticity assessments,
the ultimate locus of authenticity in this case resides in
the consumer. Together, this typology highlights that
authenticity judgments are shaped not only by factual
verification but also by consumers' expectations, schemas, feelings, and subjective reactions.
Similarly, Grayson and Martinec (2004) developed
a typology after observing that consumers perceive
authenticity even in fictional or imaginary tourist contexts (e.g., a Sherlock Holmes exhibition). Drawing on
Peirce's semiotic framework, they distinguish between
indexical and iconic authenticity, which broadly correspond to the objective and constructed distinctions,
respectively. _Indexical authenticity_ refers to authenticity
verified through factual connections between an entity and its purported origin. _Iconic authenticity_ refers
to authenticity derived from an object's resemblance
to the “real thing” rather than factual linkage—that
is, an “authentic reproduction.” For example, a document is indexically authentic if it is indeed the original
Declaration of Independence, whereas a recreation that
closely resembles the original document would be iconically authentic. Thus, indexical authenticity relies on
factual verification, whereas iconic authenticity relies on
resemblance to the original, often corresponding to consumers' expectations or schemas.
Subsequent frameworks identify similar distinctions in other consumption domains. For example, in
their analysis of the Trappist and Abbey beer brands,
Beverland et al. (2008) show that consumers attribute
authenticity through _pure authenticity_ (literal unbroken commitment to tradition and origin), _approximate_
_authenticity_ (symbolic or abstract impression of tradition), and _moral authenticity_ (perceived genuineness
of the producers). The first two types mirror objective

**The component approach to authenticity**


The component approach also seeks to organize
variation in authenticity judgments, emerging largely
from efforts to better conceptualize and measure brand
authenticity. Whereas typology frameworks take a top-­
down perspective, theoretically distinguishing forms
of authenticity based on different sources of truth,
component approaches tend to adopt a bottom-­up
perspective, empirically identifying the attributes
consumers most strongly associate with authenticity
and incorporating them into a single model. As a result,
authenticity is treated as a construct composed of
multiple related components whose salience may vary

across contexts.
Although Spiggle et al. (2012) indirectly provided an
early component-­based approach through their investigation of brand extension authenticity (BEA), Napoli
et al. (2014) were among the first to systematically organize the various associations linked to brand authenticity into a measurable scale. This effort emerged from
the recognition that many disparate factors, including
heritage, nostalgia, cultural symbolism, sincerity, craftsmanship, quality commitment, and design consistency,
were all associated with brand authenticity. Through
scale creation and empirical analyses, they identified
three dimensions—quality commitment, sincerity, and
heritage—from the wide set of factors that together form
the core definitional structure of brand authenticity.
Later frameworks adopted similar strategies for
identifying core components of authenticity. Morhart
et al. (2015) conceptualize perceived brand authenticity



as a global construct consisting of four dimensions—
continuity, credibility, integrity, and symbolism—proposing that authenticity emerges from their combined
presence. Notably, their framework also links these dimensions to Wang's (1999) typology by associating them
with objective, constructed, and existential authenticity
cues. Likewise, Akbar and Wymer (2017) identified 40
dimensions discussed in prior research and, through empirical analysis, distilled these to two components: originality and genuineness. Most recent in this approach,
Nunes et al. (2021) propose a framework designed to organize the many meanings of authenticity across marketplace contexts. Drawing on interviews, surveys, and
prior literature, they conceptualize authenticity as a
holistic assessment composed of six components—accuracy, connectedness, integrity, legitimacy, originality,
and proficiency—while noting that the relative importance of these components may vary across consumption

contexts.
In sum, the component approach preserves authenticity as a single construct while decomposing it into multiple interrelated dimensions. Most frameworks include
a component capturing linkage to origin (e.g., heritage
or continuity) and another reflecting genuine intentions
(e.g., sincerity or integrity). Many also incorporate elements related to consumers' emotional responses (e.g.,
connectedness or symbolism), and some highlight aesthetic or craftsmanship-­related qualities. Yet, perhaps
because this approach proceeds from a largely bottom-­up examination of the attributes consumers most
commonly associate with authenticity, frameworks arrive at somewhat different sets of components (e.g., some
include six, others only two).


**Summary**


Taken together, the typology and component approaches
have made significant progress in making sense of the
complex aspects of authenticity. Typology approaches
have overall taken a more top-­down perspective,
comprehensively mapping the different sources of
truth that may underlie authenticity judgments (i.e.,
objective, socially constructed, internally focused),
whereas component approaches take a more bottom-­up
perspective, identifying the associations most strongly
linked to authenticity perceptions (e.g., heritage,
originality, integrity, symbolism).
Although this prior work points to consumption contexts as influencing the variation in authenticity assessments, there has been limited explicit theorizing about
how fundamental psychological processes determine when
different aspects of authenticity are activated. A process
perspective may be particularly useful because such processes operate across all consumption contexts, enabling
more parsimonious predictions about when different authenticity judgments arise and their resulting implications

**AUTHENTICITY AS A**
**DUAL-­PROCESS JUDGMENT**


We begin from the premise that authenticity is the outcome
of a _judgment process_ . Authenticity judgments arise when
consumers seek to determine whether an entity is real, true,
or genuine—that is, an entity is _what it claims or appears_
_to be_ . Consequently, such judgments involve evaluating
whether an entity's explicit or implicit claims or attributes
align with what consumers perceive the entity to be. For
example, a bag displaying the Louis Vuitton logo implicitly
“claims” to have been manufactured by Louis Vuitton.
A consumer perceives the bag as authentic if that claim
is judged to be true and inauthentic if it is not. Although
entities can make many kinds of claims—about their
origin, heritage, quality, or moral values—the core question
remains the same: whether the entity is what it purports to
be. Accordingly, components such as originality, morality,
or quality are not inherently authentic unless the entity
claims to be original, moral, or high quality. Instead, these
components can be seen as potential claims or attributes
whose relevance depends on how consumers process them.
This shift raises key questions: What processes shape
authenticity judgments? What claims or attributes do these
processes lead consumers to attend to? What conditions
trigger one process rather than another?
While authenticity judgments may be composed of
different types and components as illustrated in the prior
section, the processes consumers use to form these judgments likely resemble those used in other evaluative
judgments. Dual-­process theories have long recognized
that individuals tend to approach information processing through two general modes: one more deliberate,
cognitive, and conscious and one more intuitive, emotional, and unconscious (e.g., Evans, 2008; Samson &
Voyer, 2012). Across consumption domains, scholars
have formalized these modalities into models, including
the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM; Petty &
Cacioppo, 1986) and the Heuristic-­Systematic Model
(HSM; Chaiken, 1980) in persuasion research; System 1
and System 2 (Kahneman, 2011) and the Experiential-­
Analytic Model (Epstein, 1994) in judgment and decision
making; and the Reflective-­Impulsive Model (Strack &
Deutsch, 2004) and Hot/Cool System (Metcalfe &
Mischel, 1999) in self-­regulation. Each distinguishes between a more deliberate route (e.g., central processing in
ELM; System 2) and a more intuitive route (e.g.,



peripheral processing in ELM; System 1). This distinction also aligns with broader work examining when individuals rely more on cognition versus affect in forming
judgments (e.g., Greifeneder et al., 2011; Hsee et al., 2015).
Although some work in authenticity has highlighted the
cognitive and affective components in authenticity judgments (e.g., Cohen & Cohen, 2012; Selwyn, 1996), predominant models in consumer research have not

explicitly theorized authenticity as the product of distinct information-­processing routes. [1]

This core recognition that judgments are made either
more deliberately or intuitively has provided conceptual
clarity across these consumption domains. Indeed, a
central motivation of dual-­process models has been to
explain why individuals deviate from normative or rational expectations (Chaiken & Ledgerwood, 2007). In
persuasion, reliance on the peripheral route explains
why consumers may fail to update their attitudes even
when faced with weak persuasive messages, instead
gravitating toward superficial cues of credibility or other
less relevant signals like a spokesperson's attractiveness
(e.g., Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). In judgment and decision
making, the heuristic-­driven thinking of System 1 helps
explain why individuals often depart from what would
objectively or economically serve their best interest
(e.g., Kahneman, 2011). Dual-­process theories also explain failures of self-­control, in which consumers choose
short-­term ease over long-­term benefits (e.g., Hofmann
et al., 2009).
Extending this logic to authenticity, the extent to
which consumers approach authenticity judgments in
a more deliberate or intuitive manner may similarly
shape the wide variation observed in such judgments.
Specifically, we propose consumers process authenticity
through two distinct pathways: a fact-­based route and a
feeling-­based route.


**Fact-­based route**


Because authenticity is typically understood to be the
quality of being real or true, one natural approach
consumers use to make such an evaluation is through
deliberate, analytical processing of a target entity's
real or true nature. We term this the fact-­based route.
Paralleling features of central processing in ELM and
System 2, this route involves controlled and effortful
evaluation. What distinguishes this route from general

**Feeling-­based route**


Consumers may also approach authenticity judgments
through a more intuitive mode, which we term the
feeling-­based route. Consistent with peripheral
processing and System 1, consumers may rely on easily
accessible cues or heuristics to assess authenticity. In line
with the affective dominance of the experiential system
of the Experiential-­Analytic Model, they may also
prioritize emotional reactions or sensory impressions

reflects the “feelings-­as-­information” framework (Avnet
et al., 2012; Pham et al., 2001; Schwarz, 1990; Schwarz



& Clore, 1988) and extensive literature showing the role
of affect in consumer judgments (for reviews, see Cohen
et al., 2007; Pham, 2004).
Notably, the overarching structure of the authenticity
judgment remains the same as in the fact-­based route:
consumers assess whether an entity is what it claims or
appears to be. What differs is the process by which they
make this assessment. Rather than analytically evaluating claims and attributes, consumers rely more heavily
on intuitive, gestalt processing, such as emotional resonance, sensory impressions, and automatic associations,
to assess the match between the entity's purported attributes and their conception of what the entity truly is. For
instance, when evaluating whether a Louis Vuitton bag is
authentic, a consumer using the feeling-­based route may
rely on the mere presence of the Louis Vuitton logo, on
surface-­level aesthetic impressions, such as whether the
bag resembles other luxury bags they have seen, or on
easily accessible contextual cues (e.g., the bag is carried
by someone who seems wealthy) to assess authenticity. In
short, the consumer relies on what “feels real.”
Prior research acknowledges affective and associative
elements in authenticity assessments, typically framing
them as distinct types or components of authenticity.

consumers' preexisting expectations, beliefs, and experiences are influential in shaping these judgments.

the features of the process through which that judgment

systematic comparison, whereas the feeling-­based route
relies on intuitive, associative processing. This difference
in processing influences which claims or attributes receive attention, thereby shaping the authenticity assessment and resulting brand outcomes.

whether the two modes are conceptualized as either-­or

various situational and motivational moderators that

propose that variation in authenticity judgments reflects
the degree to which consumers rely on the fact-­based or
feeling-­based route.

**AUTHENTICITY CUES AND**
**AUTHENTICITY JUDGMENTS**


Building on this dual-­process account, we now examine
how the fact-­based and feeling-­based routes direct
attention to different authenticity-­relevant cues and shape
the resulting authenticity judgment. We first identify
dimensions of authenticity cues and derive predictions
regarding how each route prioritizes them. We then



outline core dimensions of authenticity judgments and
propose predictions about how these routes differentially
shape those judgments.


**Attentional focus on authenticity cues**


Authenticity judgments arise when consumers try
to determine whether an entity is what it claims or
appears to be. To make this assessment, consumers
draw on a range of cues whose relevance depends on
their processing route. We propose that these cues vary
along two dimensions: (1) claim explicitness (i.e., explicit
vs. implicit claims) and (2) attribute diagnosticity (i.e.,
diagnostic vs. superficial attributes).


Claim explicitness (vs. implicitness)


Claims made by an entity (i.e., a brand, product, or
individual) can vary in whether they are communicated
explicitly or implicitly. By explicit claims we refer to
identifiable, concrete assertions made by an entity.
For example, a wine brand that claims to be produced
in France (i.e., “Made in France”) is making a direct,
verifiable assertion (i.e., either it was made in France,
or it was not). Implicit claims, by contrast, are those not
directly stated but implied. Because they are conveyed
indirectly (e.g., through imagery or stylistic cues) they are
less amenable to objective verification. For instance, a
wine brand may evoke associations with France through
visual design or symbolic elements without making an
explicit claim about its origin.
This distinction parallels the typological differentiation between pure authenticity and approximate authenticity (Beverland et al., 2008) and conceptualizations of
brand authenticity that distinguish between more verifiable attributes (e.g., continuity via a literal connection
to place over time) and stylistic or symbolic elements
(Morhart et al., 2015). However, in our framework, these
variations are not treated as types or components of authenticity, but as features of the target entity that may
be differentially prioritized depending on the consumer's
information-­processing route.
We propose that consumers adopting the fact-­based
route are more likely to focus on explicit claims as they
enable systematic comparison with objective, external
standards. By contrast, when using the feeling-­based
route, consumers may place greater emphasis on implicit
cues of authenticity. In particular, this route may direct
attention to aesthetic and sensory cues as they are generally processed rapidly (e.g., Potter et al., 2014; Thorpe
et al., 1996), with processing fluency playing a key role in
aesthetic judgments (e.g., Reber et al., 2004). Although
some aesthetic or sensory cues can, in principle, be
objectively verified, they are often not directly falsifiable. As a result, such cues are more likely to dominate

Attribute diagnosticity (vs. superficiality)


A second key dimension concerns the degree to which
an attribute of the entity is diagnostic versus superficial
in nature. We propose that diagnostic attributes are
difficult to counterfeit and provide more reliable
information about whether an entity genuinely possesses
the qualities it purports to have. Superficial attributes
are readily observable and easier to replicate, even when
the underlying authenticity is absent. Echoing signaling
theory (e.g., Connelly et al., 2011; Spence, 1973),
diagnostic attributes are costly signals that reliably
convey underlying authenticity because they are more
difficult to mimic, whereas superficial attributes are
easier to imitate and thus constitute “cheap talk.” For
example, when assessing the authenticity of a Victorian
era armoire, diagnostic cues would be those that are
difficult to counterfeit, such as period-­specific joinery
techniques or verifiable provenance documentation.



Conversely, superficial attributes (e.g., visible scratches,
worn surfaces, or patina) are easily fabricated and may
merely create the appearance of an authentic antique.
It is worth distinguishing diagnosticity from explicitness. Explicitness refers to the way in which a claim
is conveyed (directly stated versus implied), whereas
diagnosticity refers to the extent to which an attribute
is difficult to fake. A claim or attribute may be explicit
yet superficial (e.g., a prominently displayed but easily
faked establishment-­date sign) or implicit yet diagnostic
(e.g., subtle construction details difficult to replicate).
We propose that consumers engaging in a fact-­based
route are more likely to attend to attributes that are
difficult to counterfeit and thus more diagnostic of authenticity. This is because they are more likely to scrutinize discrete cues of authenticity and compare them
to perceived verifiable standards. By contrast, because
the feeling-­based route is characterized by intuitive and
rapid processing, consumers relying on this route tend to
attend to attributes that are immediately observable and
cognitively accessible, likely directing their attention to
superficial attributes that are more susceptible to imitation and manipulation. We predict:


**H2a.** When assessing the authenticity of a
target entity, consumers engaging in the fact-­
based (vs. feeling-­based) route will attend
more to diagnostic (vs. superficial) attributes.

**Authenticity judgment characteristics**


We propose that the fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route's
focus on claim explicitness (vs. implicitness) and attribute
diagnosticity (vs. superficiality) of the entity, in turn,
shapes key characteristics of the resulting authenticity
judgments of the entity. Below, we identify three higher-­
order dimensions of authenticity judgments—epistemic
validity, social consensus, and structural form—and
make predictions about how they are shaped by these
proposed routes.



Epistemic validity


One core feature of an authenticity judgment is its
epistemic validity, which refers to the extent to which a
judgment corresponds to objective reality—in essence,
its accuracy. For instance, if a consumer judges a Louis
Vuitton bag to be authentic and it is in fact authentic,
the judgment has high epistemic validity; if the bag is
inauthentic, the judgment has low epistemic validity.
Consistent with typologies recognizing that types of
authenticity align with objective reality (e.g., Moulard
et al., 2021; Wang, 1999) or component approaches
highlighting accuracy (Nunes et al., 2021), authenticity
judgments can be assessed based on the extent to which
they accord with verifiable facts outside of the consumer.
However, we do not conceptualize epistemic validity as
a distinct type or component of authenticity. Rather, we
argue that all authenticity judgments are perceptions of
realness, but these perceptions may systematically vary
in epistemic validity depending on the processing route
through which they are formed.
We propose that judgments formed via the fact-­based
route are more likely to be higher in epistemic validity.
Consistent with H1a and H2a, this is because consumers
are more likely to attend to explicit claims or diagnostic
attributes that tend to be more consistent with objectively verifiable criteria. Although such processing does
not guarantee accuracy, it increases the likelihood that
authenticity judgments align with externally confirmable
evidence. By contrast, judgments formed via the feeling-­
based route rely on implicit claims (H1a) and superficial
attributes (H2a), which are more closely tied to affective
reactions, sensory impressions, and intuitive associations.
These processes tend to be less tied to verifiable evidence
and therefore less consistently reflect objective reality.
However, we do not suggest that intuitive judgments are
inherently inaccurate. Research on thin-­slicing shows that
individuals can form surprisingly accurate assessments
from minimal information (Ambady & Rosenthal, 1992;
Willis & Todorov, 2006). Further, reliance on feelings can
be adaptive or “logical” (Pham, 2004). From an evolutionary perspective, rapid affective assessments may be well-­
calibrated for interpersonal contexts, particularly when
individuals infer social or relational qualities (e.g., sincerity) from perceived authenticity. In this sense, authenticity
can have a “know-­it-­when-­you-­see-­it” quality, suggesting
that consumers may sometimes accurately recognize (in)
authenticity through immediate intuitive impressions
rather than deliberate analysis. Nevertheless, because such
judgments are not systematically anchored in verifiable evidence, their epistemic validity is likely to vary more widely
than judgments derived from the fact-­based route. Taken
together, we posit:


**H3.** Authenticity judgments formed via the
fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route will exhibit greater epistemic validity.

Social consensus


Another key dimension of authenticity judgments is the
degree of social consensus—the extent to which consumers
agree that a target entity is authentic. Although related to
epistemic validity, the two are distinct: epistemic validity
concerns whether a judgment is objectively true, whereas
social consensus reflects the degree of intersubjective
agreement regarding shared meanings or standards (Wan
et al., 2010). Thus, authenticity judgments may vary not
only in accuracy but also in how widely they are shared.
Prior work implicitly treats social consensus as central
to certain types of authenticity. Constructed, type, and
true-­to-­ideal authenticity all suggest that authenticity
depends on alignment with category membership or societally shared standards (e.g., Carroll & Wheaton, 2009;
Moulard et al., 2021; Wang, 1999). For instance, whether
a Mexican dish is authentic depends on socially shared
standards rather than on fixed truth. We conceptualize social consensus as a cross-­cutting dimension of
authenticity judgments that varies systematically with
consumers' processing routes. Anticipating consensus
is particularly valuable for marketers seeking to predict
broad consumer reactions, as we discuss in the brand
outcomes section.
We propose that authenticity judgments formed via
the fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route are more likely
to possess greater social consensus. When engaging in
fact-­based processing, consumers rely on explicit claims
that often have externally verifiable standards (H1a).
Because these standards are often shared and perceived
as objective, they reduce interpretive variability, increasing the likelihood that authenticity judgments converge
across consumers. For example, consumers may evaluate
the authenticity of Mexican cuisine using commonly accepted indicators of authenticity, such as a dish's historical continuity or ingredients sourced from the country of
origin. By contrast, feeling-­based processing relies on the
emotional reactions, sensory impressions, and personal
associations elicited by implicit claims (H1a) or superficial attributes (H2a). Cues like the music playing in the
restaurant or the weathered look of the menu may evoke
impressions of Mexican tradition, leading to inferences
of authenticity. Because these reactions are more likely to
vary across individuals, authenticity judgments formed
through this route are less likely to lead to intersubjective agreement. That said, feeling-­based processing can
sometimes produce social consensus: when consumers
share similar schemas or cultural experiences, affective
responses may indeed align. However, because such consensus depends on subjective experience rather than on
more stable, externally anchored standards, it is less consistently achieved. Taken together, we posit:


**H4.** Authenticity judgments formed via the
fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route will exhibit greater social consensus.



Structural form


Another dimension of authenticity judgments is
structural form—the degree to which authenticity
judgments are categorical (i.e., binary in nature) versus
continuous. Although this dimension has yet to be
clearly delineated as a feature of authenticity judgments,
we propose that it is meaningful as it helps predict when
consumers will accept deviations in authenticity. As we
detail in the brand outcomes section, binary judgments
likely lead to harsher negative reactions from consumers,
because they draw a clearer line between authentic and
inauthentic, whereas continuous judgments may allow
for greater tolerance of deviations.
We reason that authenticity judgments stemming from
the fact-­based route are more likely to be categorical.
Because this route directs attention to explicit, falsifiable
claims (H1a), entities are more likely to be evaluated as
either meeting or failing to meet objective criteria. For
example, a consumer may categorize Converse's Chuck
Taylor All Star shoes as inauthentic after any change to
the 100-­year-­old shoe design because the objective link
between the design and its origin has been broken. This
all-­or-­nothing structure parallels prior work on purity
and categorical evaluation (Newman, 2016), in which
violations undermine authenticity entirely rather than
incrementally. By contrast, consumers engaging in the
feeling-­based route may perceive authenticity along a gradient, with entities feeling more or less authentic depending on the strength and coherence of affective or sensory
impressions. Because this route emphasizes implicit, less
falsifiable claims (H1a), authenticity is experienced more
as a matter of degree. For instance, a consumer may
judge updated Chuck Taylor All Star shoes as _somewhat_
authentic because the overall style and feel of the shoe are
relatively similar to original. We predict:


**H5.** Authenticity judgments formed via the
fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route will be
more categorical (vs. continuous) in structural form.


In sum, we propose that consumers relying on the
fact-­based route will be more likely to attend to explicit
claims of the entity (H1a) and diagnostic cues (H2a). As
a result, authenticity judgments exhibit higher epistemic
validity (H3), social consensus (H4), and a more binary
structure (H5). By contrast, the feeling-­based route leads
consumers to focus on implicit claims of the entity (H1a)
and superficial cues (H2a). Consequently, authenticity
judgments exhibit lower epistemic validity (H3), greater
idiosyncrasy (H4), and a more continuous structure (H5).
We have also proposed that claim explicitness (H1b) and
attribute diagnosticity (H2b) may serve as antecedents
that activate alternative processing routes. The next section examines situational and motivational moderators
that may induce reliance on these routes.

**SITUATIONAL AND**
**MOTIVATIONAL MODERATORS**


Relatively little systematic work has examined the
situational and motivational moderators that shape
when and how consumers assess authenticity. This gap is
perhaps largely due to the construct of authenticity itself,
which has required substantial conceptual unpacking.
By focusing on fundamental psychological processes
underlying authenticity judgments, our framework helps
identify moderators that may trigger different processing

routes.


**Situational moderators**


We begin with situational moderators that emerge
from the recognition that consumers can approach
authenticity judgments through fact-­based or feeling-­
based processing: perceived risk, perceived ability or
opportunity, and domain expertise.


Perceived risk


An underexplored facet of authenticity judgments is that
they do not carry equal stakes. Some entail meaningful financial, physical, or social consequences if they are inaccurate (H3), whereas others are relatively inconsequential.
In keeping with foundational work showing that processing
routes depend on personal relevance (e.g., Petty et al., 1981,
1983), we propose that the perceived risk associated with an
authenticity judgment shapes the processing route consumers employ. When the stakes are high, consumers are more
likely to engage in effortful verification and hence use the
fact-­based route. When the stakes are low, they can afford
to rely on less effortful, feeling-­based processing. For example, a consumer considering a $25,000 painting is likely
to rely on the fact-­based route to verify that the painting is
genuinely produced by the purported artist, given the high
cost of being incorrect. By contrast, a consumer spending
$10 on a meal at a restaurant believed to be an authentic
hole-­in-­the-­wall Chinese spot faces minimal consequences
if mistaken and may rely on aesthetic cues and overall
“vibes” to judge authenticity. We posit:


**H6.** When an authenticity assessment involves high (vs. low) perceived risk, consumers are more likely to rely on the fact-­based
(vs. feeling-­based) route in forming their authenticity judgments.


Perceived ability or opportunity


Consumers can vary in whether they perceive that they
have the ability or opportunity (i.e., cognitive resources



and the available time) to consider the authenticity
of a marketplace entity. Research in the ELM and the
MAO framework (motivation, ability, opportunity)
demonstrates that ability and opportunity reliably
shape the depth of information processing (MacInnis
& Jaworski, 1989; Maclnnis et al., 1991; Petty &
Cacioppo, 1986; Shiv & Fedorikhin, 1999). Although
dual-­process theories widely recognize MAO factors as
drivers of processing depth, these antecedents have not
been applied to authenticity judgments. Consistent with
this perspective, we propose that ability and opportunity
influence the processing route consumers employ when
evaluating authenticity claims. When consumers have
sufficient cognitive resources and time, they are better
able to engage in fact-­based processing. By contrast,
when time is perceived to be constrained or cognitive
load is high, consumers are more likely to rely on intuitive
impressions and surface cues. For example, a parent
shopping under time pressure may rely on packaging
cues or general “healthy” aesthetics (H1a, H2a) to
judge whether a product is authentically organic. A
consumer with ample time may examine ingredient lists,
certifications, and company practices to verify organic
authenticity claims. We propose:


**H7.** When consumers have high (vs. low)
perceived ability or opportunity to consider
authenticity, they are more likely to rely on
the fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route in
forming their authenticity judgments.


Domain expertise


Domain expertise likely shapes how consumers assess
authenticity. Expertise is typically acquired through
deliberate practice (Ericsson et al., 1993), reflecting fact-­
based processing. As consumers develop expertise, they
engage in careful study of the attributes that distinguish
authentic from inauthentic entities (H2a). For example,
art experts gain detailed knowledge through extensive
analysis of artworks. Through this training, experts
come to appreciate the value of assessing authenticity
via a fact-­based route. That said, there may be scenarios
in which experts appropriately rely on feeling-­based
processing, as their intuitive judgments reflect well-­
calibrated schemas developed through prior exposure
(Alba & Hutchinson, 1987). An art expert who has viewed
many authentic Picassos may know when authenticity
can be accurately assessed from quick impressions.
Importantly, expertise also enables recognition of when
observable cues are insufficient and deeper, fact-­based
assessment is required, as it improves the calibration of
domain knowledge (Alba & Hutchinson, 1987, 2000).
Thus, while domain expertise may lead to more accurate
intuitive judgments, these judgments are honed through
fact-­based processing. Taken together, consumers with

greater domain expertise will be more likely to rely on
fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) processing when assessing
authenticity.


**H8.** When consumers have high (vs. low)
domain expertise, they are more likely to rely
on the fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route in
forming their authenticity judgments.


**Motivational moderators**


Relatively little work has examined how consumer motivations predict the desire for authenticity or the form it
takes. Beverland and Farrelly (2010), drawing on qualitative analysis of consumer interviews, identified three
motivations for authenticity: control, connection, and

certain authentic products. Building on this work, we
theorize how the fact-­based and feeling-­based routes accord with self-­determination theory (SDT), a prominent
theory of human motivation closely linked to authenticity (e.g., Kernis & Goldman, 2006; Ryan & Deci, 2000).
Self-­determination theory proposes three core motivations—autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci
& Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2020)—which we propose
shape consumer reliance on fact-­based versus feeling-­
based processing when evaluating authenticity.


Need for autonomy


Need for autonomy refers to the need to feel that one's
actions are self-­driven and not due to external pressure
(Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020). We propose that this need can shape whether consumers rely
on fact-­based or feeling-­based processing. Specifically,
a high need for autonomy heightens sensitivity to internal states, increasing reliance on internal experiential cues when assessing authenticity. In other words, a
high need for autonomy may increase people's tendency
to lean on _themselves_ as the determinant of authenticity. For example, when engaged in a tourist experience, consumers high in autonomy may focus on how
the experience makes them feel to determine whether
it is authentic. The feeling-­based route may also appeal
to those who desire autonomy because it may result in
an authenticity judgment that feels more unique or idiosyncratic to them rather than conforming to others'
assessments (H4). Conversely, consumers with a low
need for autonomy may rely more heavily on external
markers to determine authenticity, consistent with the
fact-­based route.


**H9.** Consumers with a high (vs. low) need
for autonomy will be more likely to employ



the feeling-­based (vs. fact-­based) route when
assessing authenticity.


Need for competence


Need for competence refers to the need to feel that one's
actions are effective and capable of producing desired
outcomes (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020).
While autonomy heightens attention to internal experiential states, competence emphasizes effectiveness, performance, and externally verifiable success. Individuals
with a high need for competence are motivated to confirm that their actions and choices are objectively valid
and efficacious. Thus, they may be more likely to engage
in the fact-­based route, relying on external markers that
can be objectively verified when determining whether
something is authentic (H1a, H2a) to arrive at a more
accurate assessment (H3). For example, in a tourist context, consumers with a high need for competence may
evaluate authenticity based on whether the experience
corresponds with historically accurate features, which is
consistent with the fact-­based route. However, consumers with a low need for competence may place relatively
less weight on such factors. Thus:


**H10.** Consumers with a high (vs. low) need
for competence will be more likely to employ
a fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route when assessing authenticity.


Need for relatedness


Need for relatedness, or the desire to feel connected to
others (Niemiec & Ryan, 2009; Ryan & Deci, 2020),
may shape how consumers form authenticity judgments.
Because fact-­based processing tends to lead to assessments
that exhibit higher social consensus (H4), consumers seeking relatedness may rely on this route to enhance intersubjective agreement. Supporting this view, consumers with a
high need to belong respond more positively to diagnostic,
falsifiable cues that signal a direct link between a product and a valued individual (Newman & Smith 2016b),
implying greater reliance on fact-­based processing (H2a).
However, a need for relatedness could move a consumer
toward feeling-­based processing when doing so would
create greater social connection. For instance, the need
to belong can lead consumers to emphasize their experiential states in pursuit of authentic connection (Beverland
& Farrelly, 2010). More broadly, consumers may engage
in motivated reasoning (Cohen, 2003; Kunda, 1990), construing authenticity in ways that support affiliation over
verifiable standards. Taken together, prior work does not
definitively establish whether a need for relatedness promotes reliance on fact-­based or feeling-­based processing.
On balance, because a need for relatedness centers on

intersubjective judgment, we speculate that consumers
with a high need for relatedness will generally rely more on
fact-­based processing, as it is more likely to yield socially
shared standards:


**H11.** Consumers with a high (vs. low) need
for relatedness will be more likely to employ
the fact-­based (vs. feeling-­based) route when
assessing authenticity.


**BRAND OUTCOMES**


Reliance on fact-­based versus feeling-­based processing
of authenticity has important implications for brand
outcomes. Prior research has largely emphasized positive
effects of authenticity, such as brand trust and valuation.
We extend this work by theorizing how differences in
authenticity processing shape both positive and negative
outcomes, including consumer loyalty, brand reputational
risk, innovation flexibility, and consumer fragmentation.


**Consumer loyalty**


One key outcome for brands is the degree to
which consumers exhibit loyalty due to perceived
authenticity. We propose that the more consumers
arrive at their authenticity beliefs via the fact-­based
(vs. feeling-­based) route, the greater the loyalty the
brand will garner. This reasoning mirrors prior work
on the ELM, which demonstrates that consumers who
engage in central processing of a persuasive message
tend to form stronger attitudes than those who rely
on peripheral processing (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986).
Consumers relying on the fact-­based route prioritize
explicit claims (H1a) and diagnostic attributes (H2a).
Consequently, they form authenticity assessments
that are more epistemically grounded (H3) and
characterized by greater social consensus (H4). As a
result, brands that achieve authenticity through this
route (H1b; H2b) are more likely to gain loyalty from a
broader, more predictable segment of the population.
Conversely, when consumers rely on the feeling-­based
route to ascribe authenticity, their judgments are more
idiosyncratic (H4), affect-­driven, and less elaborated
upon as they default to implicit claims (H1a) and
superficial attributes (H2a). Thus, such authenticity
assessments may produce weaker and less stable
positive attitudes, resulting in lower levels of loyalty.


**H12.** Conditional on authenticity being
affirmed, consumers who employ the fact-­
based (vs. feeling-­based) route will exhibit
higher loyalty toward the target brand.



**Risk of brand reputational harm**


Although consumers using the fact-­based route may
reward brands with greater loyalty when authenticity
is affirmed, they may also impose stronger backlash
when authenticity is disconfirmed. Because fact-­based
processing produces authenticity judgments that are
more binary (H5) and epistemically grounded (H3),
brands processed via this route may face greater scrutiny.
As a result, brands must ensure that the objective quality
of their offerings substantiates their explicit claims. If
such claims are revealed to be false, brands are more
likely to be accused of deception or misrepresentation,
leading to heightened reputational costs.
Authenticity judgments formed via the feeling-­based
route rely more on implicit claims (H1a), which tend to
be aesthetic or sensory. The less falsifiable nature of these
cues may reduce the likelihood of direct accusations of dishonesty when inconsistencies arise. In this sense, brands
processed through the feeling-­based route may be buffered
from severe backlash. For example, in cases of greenwashing, brands may use sensory cues (e.g., green-­colored packaging or nature imagery) to indirectly signal environmental
responsibility, regardless of a product's actual environmental impact. Because such cues are less falsifiable (H2a) and
result in authenticity judgments that fall on a gradient
rather than a binary (H5), consumers relying on the feeling-­
based route may exhibit weaker punitive responses.


**H13.** Conditional on authenticity being disconfirmed, consumers who employ the fact-­
based (vs. feeling-­based) route will impose
greater backlash on the target brand.


**Innovation flexibility**


Prior work suggests that one cost of positioning a
brand as authentic is increased resistance to product
innovation, as changes may be seen as violating
authenticity (Han et al., 2021). These effects are
especially pronounced for brands whose authenticity
is tied to explicit, falsifiable cues, such as claims of
consistency over time (e.g., a product has been made
the same way for 50 years). When consumers employ the
fact-­based route to assess authenticity, they are more
likely to anchor their judgments in explicit claims (H1a)
and diagnostic attributes (H2a). As a result, deviations
from these claims, such as updates to longstanding
flagship products, may be seen as authenticity violations,
leading to greater resistance to innovation. By contrast,
authenticity judgments formed via the feeling-­based
route rely more on implicit claims (H1a). Because such
claims are less bound to falsifiable standards, brands
processed through this route may face fewer constraints
when adapting to trends or introducing innovation.

**H14.** Consumers who employ the feeling-­
based (vs. fact-­based) route when assessing
authenticity will exhibit greater acceptance
of product innovation or change.


**Consumer fragmentation**


Although innovation and change may be less likely to
disrupt authenticity for consumers engaging in feeling-­
based processing, authenticity judgments formed
through this route are also more heterogeneous across
individuals. Because authenticity derived from the feeling-­
based route is more linked to consumers' intuitions and

emotions, consumers may rely on cues that lack socially
agreed-­upon meanings (H4), leading to divergent
views of authenticity and consumer fragmentation.
Consequently, brands evaluated through the feeling-­
based route may face greater difficulty forecasting which
attributes consumers will perceive as authentic and may
need to offer a broader range of products or make more
frequent tactical adjustments to accommodate dispersed
preferences. For example, a beauty brand that claims its
products “make you feel like your true self” may need
to provide a wide range of colors and shades or closely
track beauty trends to anticipate varied interpretations
of authenticity.


**H15.** Consumers who employ the feeling-­
based (vs. fact-­based) route when assessing
authenticity will be less predictable in their
judgments and related brand preferences.


Taken together, brands processed through the fact-­
based route face a higher risk, higher reward dynamic
with respect to authenticity. These brands may need to
invest more resources to ensure their claims are objectively supported. If they fail to do so, they are likely to
face strong consumer backlash (H13). They also may be
less able to innovate or deviate far from their authenticity claims (H14). However, if brands fulfill their objective
claims, they will likely experience greater rewards in the
form of consumer loyalty (H12) as well as stronger consumer consensus and predictability in authenticity perceptions and other claimed attributes (H4; H15).
Brands processed through the feeling-­based route
may face lower risk but also receive fewer rewards for
their authenticity. Because consumers relying on the
feeling-­based route are less likely to attend to explicit,
diagnostic claims, brands are less likely to be accused
of inauthenticity and thus are less likely to experience
consumer backlash (H13). Relatedly, these brands may
require less resources to support their authenticity
claims. They also may be more able to nimbly adapt and
innovate with trends without risking authenticity (H14).
However, because they rely on superficial cues of authenticity, brands are also less likely to gain consumer



loyalty (H12) and may struggle to gain consensus across
consumers (H4; H15) as they rely on consumers' idiosyncratic and potentially fickle preferences to achieve authenticity, often requiring more diversified offerings to
accommodate varied preferences.


**CONCLUSION**


Authenticity has long been recognized as a highly desired
yet elusive property in the marketplace. Consequently, numerous frameworks have emerged documenting its types
and components. Rather than further specifying what authenticity is, we shift the focus to how consumers make
authenticity judgments. We offer a simplified, integrative
framework in which authenticity is the result of a judgment process that proceeds through two distinct routes:
a fact-­based route and a feeling-­based route. By identifying psychological processes as a key source of variation,
our framework reconciles longstanding paradoxes in the
literature and enables greater conceptual integration by
connecting authenticity to well-­established theories in
consumer behavior and psychology. We also show how
this framework predicts the authenticity cues consumers
attend to, the characteristics of the judgments, and the
moderators that activate these processes. From a managerial perspective, this approach highlights a central strategic
question for brands: when consumers evaluate authenticity, will they rely on facts or feelings?
