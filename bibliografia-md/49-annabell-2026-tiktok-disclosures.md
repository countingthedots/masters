interpretations of legal frameworks and demonstrate how regulation is strategically
wielded to establish legitimacy in the creator economy by specific influencers. In doing
so, I conceptually and empirically advance work on calling out practices (Geusens
et al., 2023; Lawson, 2021; Lee & Abidin, 2024; Lewis & Christin, 2022; Nakamura,

in thinking through the intersection of public accountability and shaming. Lack of dis­

**Governing the creator economy**


The creator economy refers to the ecosystem where influencers generate income through
the monetisation of content creation. This bourgeoning industry is projected to be worth
$480 billion by 2027 (Goldman Sachs, 2023), especially thanks to influencer marketing.
Different actors attempt to manage and structure the actions of influencers through the
exercise of authority, establishing rules, expectations, and obligations, implicitly and
explicitly, formally and informally. This includes platforms that regulate influencers
through their policies, interface designs, monetisation programmes, and monitoring
and enforcement processes (Annabell et al., 2025; Caplan & Gillespie, 2020; Kopf,
2022). It also involves states and regulators through legal frameworks applicable to the
activities of influencers, such as consumer and media law (Goanta & Ranchordás,
2020), along with brands and intermediaries like talent managers.
While these forms of institutional power intervene in the working lives of influencers,
engender dependencies, and crystallise inequalities (see, for example, Glatt, 2024),
influencers and their audiences also develop self-governance practices to push back.
For example, YouTube creators debate moderation policies and practices (Hallinan
et al., 2025), and the implementation of copyright law on the platform (Hallinan et al.,
2024; Kaye & Gray, 2021). Hallinan et al. (2024) distinguish between horizontal callouts,
where creators address other creators and invoke the shared value of community, and
vertical callouts, where creators address platforms, appealing to fairness. As they demon­
strate, efforts to enforce formal and informal rules in the creator economy through call­
ing out are a form of governance.


**Calling out and critiquing influencer behaviour**


Calling out as ‘discursive accountability practice’ can be traced back to Black oral tra­
ditions (Clark, 2020, p. 89), in which, in the practice of Black women, there is ‘a critique
of systemic inequality rather than an attack against specific, individualistic transgres­
sions’ (Brock, 2020, p. 220). Work on calling out in the creator economy coalesces around
the intersection of influencer behaviour with patriarchal structures, racism, and consu­
mer capitalism. Rather than calling outs of brands (Lawson, 2021), most research
addresses callouts amongst influencers (Geusens et al., 2023; Lewis & Christin, 2022)
or audiences (Duffy et al., 2022; Lee & Abidin, 2024). The latter speaks to the volatility
between influencers and audiences, in which Gurrieri et al. (2023) caution against under­
estimating the power and impact that the latter can hold.

For Clark (2020, p. 88), the mobilisation of ‘useful anger by minoritised people and
groups’ has transformed into a social media ‘strategy for networked framing of extant
social problems.’ As a result, demanding accountability through calling out has been
co-opted and hollowed out in ‘cancel culture.’ This is partially evidenced by the emer­
gence of callout as a content genre. Geusens et al. (2023) highlight how accountability
is emphasised in callout videos, which can overshadow how jealousy and business inter­
ests often drive these acts. Calling out can be used to serve interests that go beyond

**Regulating influencers’ commercial content**


When influencers engage in influencer marketing by receiving compensation, whether
money, commissions, free goods, or services, from a third party in exchange for creating
content, they produce commercial content (see also Stoldt et al., 2019). Irrespective of
whether this is called branded content or sponsored posts, it constitutes a form of adver­
tising. Unlike paid platform ads that are labelled by platforms as ads or promoted, influen­
cer ads are self-disclosed, requiring influencers to separate their commercial content from
organic content. This is part of platform governance (Annabell et al., 2024) and often a
legal requirement under consumer and media law: consumers must be clearly informed
about the existence of a commercial relationship between the influencer and the brand.

A strand of advertising and communication research aims to understand how disclos­
ures contribute to this goal of transparency. Studies on how people perceive different
types of disclosures and their effects on persuasion (Boerman, 2020; Evans et al., 2017;
Pergelova & Hachey, 2023) offer mixed results about how effective disclosures are.
This foregrounds the issue of advertising literacy and, importantly, suggests that mean­
ings can be attached to disclosures, but it does not examine how people interpret them.
There is evidence that disclosures may ‘protect’ users by activating recognition of the per­
suasive intent behind content, but we lack understanding of what disclosures mean to
users when they encounter content on platforms. Insights into what disclosures mean
for users as they encounter content on platforms.

Other work suggests that the focus on disclosure should shift toward the broader issue
of influencer marketing ethics. Wellman et al. (2020) found that influencers use authen­
ticity as a guiding ethical framework to shape their sponsored content, viewing their legal
obligation to disclose as necessary only to avoid negative consequences. In a similar vein,
Borchers and Enke (2022) highlighted that influencers see disclosures as unnecessary in
their communication because of their focus on transparency and followers’ expectations
of truthfulness in all messages. These studies suggest that disclosure can be both concep­
tually anchored to authentic, transparent influencer practices and minimised as a legal
requirement that is not essential when applying ethical principles in their content.

Although based on the US and German contexts, this perspective may help explain
why influencers may not disclose commercial content. The European Commission
(2024) found low disclosure rates through its sweep of 22 member states, supporting aca­
demic research. Within computer science, the detection of undisclosed ads is a machinelearning task in which models can be trained and developed (Bertaglia et al., 2023; Kim

et al., 2021; Zarei et al., 2020). This assumes features like brand tags and cues can be
decoded to identify undisclosed commercial content. In part, this resonates with Hall’s
(1980) model: producers embed meaning within the content through the economic
and technical infrastructure of production, while audiences decode content using per­

intended message. I argue that this can help us understand how some influencer content
is interpreted as undisclosed ads, the meanings assigned to disclosure labels, and how

influencers and audiences recognise undisclosed ads.

When it comes to the intersection of calling out and disclosure practices among
influencers, Lee and Abidin (2024) examine how advertorials are linked to cancel culture.
They trace the circulation of gossip across platforms and outlets after media reports
revealed undisclosed commercial content from two YouTube influencers in Korea.
Their analysis of user comments shows a weaponisation of calling out and cancelling
where ‘misogyny, fat-shaming, and insults’ (p. 418) were justified as ‘an act of social jus­
tice’ (p. 419). Building on insights from this case, I explore how undisclosed ads are called
out on TikTok, focusing on how the issue of disclosure is framed and connected to gen­
dered views of influencers, as well as negotiated as governance through the ‘successful’
decoding of undisclosed ads and monitoring of commercial content by different actors.


**Research design**


To explore how undisclosed influencer ads are called out on TikTok, I identify and analyse
narratives in TikTok videos and comments. I adopted a purposive sampling technique,
using keyword searches, including hidden, undisclosed and non-disclosed influencer ads,
sponsored and branded content, influencer marketing, and recommended content through
TikTok’s For You Page on a new TikTok account to curate a dataset of callout videos. This
prioritised videos focused on disclosure and references to a different legal framework that
were analytically rich in terms of video and comment content. Working with 14 videos
from six influencers allows for in-depth analysis of the callouts and their comments.

**Influencers as decoders and governors of commercial content**


On TikTok, some influencers position themselves as governors within the creator econ­
omy who are protecting audiences by drawing attention to the presence of undisclosed
ads on the platform. To do this, they create callout videos explaining why disclosure mat­
ters, identifying and criticising undisclosed or improperly disclosed commercial content
shared by other influencers, who are portrayed as exemplifying and even representing
poor industry practices.

Across the 14 callout videos, two modes of interpretation are present: undisclosed ads
as illegal and misleading, and undisclosed ads as immoral and unethical. While these
constructions co-occur, I separate them for analysis to clarify their differences.


_**Undisclosed ads illegal**_


The repeated claims by influencers that undisclosed ads are ‘illegal’ set up the seriousness
of disclosure practices. By framing disclosure as a legal requirement and citing specific
laws and authorities such as the UK’s ASA, the US’s Federal Trade Commission, and
Canada’s Competition Bureau, influencers lend legitimacy to these frameworks and
align themselves with these agencies as trustworthy protectors. Consumer protection
[language is often employed; Alice (2024a, see Supplemental data) describes undisclosed](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
[ads as ‘very misleading,’ and Bee Better (2024a, see Supplemental data) states that con­](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
sumer laws address ‘fraudulent, deceptive marketing tactics that may influence their buy­
ing and purchasing decisions.’ These legal standards are portrayed as the benchmark for
best practices as influencers evaluate undisclosed ads against official rules, endorsing this
governance system. It also helps influencers establish themselves as experts with legal
knowledge within the creator industry, reinforcing their self-image as informal regulators
who help oversee influencer activity.

Due to their positions in the industry, all influencers are assumed to be aware of laws
[that apply or, as Alice (2024a, see Supplemental data) puts it, ‘what their responsibilities](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
are in terms of disclosure.’ The educational value of the callout is for the unknowing
audience, constructed in opposition to the informed influencer who is intentionally evad­
ing their obligations, as Bee Better (2024a, see [Supplemental data) makes clear: ‘I’m](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
speaking to the audience now, not the influencers, because they’re already well aware
of this.’ Because of their informed status, undisclosed ads reflect the professionalism

undisclosed ads, this commercial content is assumed to be hidden and invisible to others,

ability but also taps into gendered moral panics and perpetuates the debasement of
imagined audience arguments put forward in media research. Influencer followers
are, therefore, viewed as being exploited financially and naïve, unable to resist persua­

Yet, the audience is also encouraged to actively participate in the governance system.
[Alice (2024c, see Supplemental data) models how to report undisclosed ads to the ASA,](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
using a screen recording of her reporting content by Amelia Liana. While she admits that
‘no one has the time to report every undisclosed ad that we see; we would be here all day,’
she legitimises reporting as a responsible action by audiences. Crucially, in this case, Alice
constructs the audience as discerning, expecting them to recognise undisclosed ads and
feel empowered through her legal expertise. Due to the high rates of undisclosed ads,
which are presented as shared knowledge, instances that irritate viewers or ‘really get
your goat’ should influence how they choose what to report. This shows how personal
feelings about specific influencers bleed into discussions of disclosure as governance.
But what is missing is a consideration of how this monitoring might be unevenly distrib­
uted among influencers, intersect with a gendered backlash, and potentially be weapo­
nised (Meisner, 2023), raising questions about what responsible governance looks like
for influencers engaged in calling out.

_**Undisclosed ads as immoral**_


Influencers also consider undisclosed ads as problematic beyond legality, emphasising
that disclosure is an ethical issue and can be considered ‘morally wrong’ (Lacey,
2024b, see [Supplemental data). When disclosures are viewed as reflecting both pro­](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
fessional and personal identity, there is a slippage between discussing the issue of undi­
sclosed ads and making a normative judgment about influencers. Alice (2024a, see
[Supplemental data), in analysing Chris Olsen’s undisclosed ad within his cultivation of](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
parasocial relationships, argues he is ‘essentially lying,’ which she says is ‘an erosion of
trust.’ She considers it deceptive because of the lack of transparency about the content’s
nature (i.e., his trip to Australia was a paid promotion for Tourism Australia). Addition­
ally, framing the situation as impulsive conceals the planning and organisation behind
brand trips. Alice points to comments under his content, showing how some users felt
guilty about not being able to afford such trips or travel spontaneously. Here, Alice
links deception to harm, suggesting that such feelings might have been avoided if the
content had been disclosed as an ad.


In decoding undisclosed ads as deceptive, some influencers refer to the underlying
motivations behind this practice:


They’re influencers who will look you in the eye through this camera hole and will literally
lie to your face about the fact that this product changed their life when it is, in fact, a brand
deal in order to weaponise the audience that they have and manipulate you into moving tens
[of millions of dollars’ worth of product. (Bee Better, 2024a, see Supplemental data)](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)


Can you imagine lying to 15.5 million people every single day and having absolutely no
remorse about it? I guarantee your answer is probably no, because any honest person
[would feel very guilty about doing that. (Lacey, 2024a, see Supplemental data)](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)


The influencer, imagined by Bee Better and hypothetical but described using explicit
references to Mikayla by Lacey, is assumed to be driven by commercial interests and
emerges as a manipulative, money-hungry figure. In the context of who is being called
out, this construction reinforces stereotypes of deceitful, superficial, and insincere
work, which are gender-coded (Duffy et al., 2022).

The understanding of the influencer is linked with the audience. Lacey separates the
‘you’ as the imagined ‘honest person’ from the influencer who lies ‘with no remorse’ or
guilt. Bee Better, on the other hand, emphasises the gullible, passive audience discussed
earlier, implying that ‘you’ are manipulated into buying what influencers promote.
Robert (2023a) offers an alternative perspective on the relationship between disclosure,
audiences, and ethical practices. In encouraging the beauty TikTok community to dis­
close ads, he proposes audiences ‘will be fine with it. When it gets annoying is when
you don’t disclose it, cause that’s pure dishonesty.’ Dishonesty is only attributed to undi­
sclosed ads, not to the creation of commercial content. This raises the issue of how people
interact with ads, hinting at why influencers may not disclose ads: users scroll past when
they recognise content as an ad (thanks to disclosure), which negatively impacts engage­
ment metrics for the ad and thus, their market value. In the analysed callout videos,
reasons for nondisclosure are limited to unethical, immoral, and deliberately non-com­
pliant attitudes and actions of influencers.

_**Decoding undisclosed ads**_


Across the callout videos, influencers present visual and verbal evidence that specific
influencers are not correctly disclosing content as advertisements. TikTok’s Stitch fea­
ture, overlaid screenshots, or embedded screen recordings are used to show the audience
‘proof’ of undisclosed commercial content as the influencer explains how it should be
decoded as an ad. For example, Bee Better (2024a, see [Supplemental data) circles in](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
red #Keihlspartner within the screenshot of Haley’s TikTok video, narrating, ‘you can
see in the very tippy top right corner in microscopic lettering, literally the smallest
text size that TikTok would physically allow somebody to do.’ Like most callouts, Bee
Better points to #partner as a clear sign of a commercial relationship, and aligns himself
with regulatory authorities also concerned with where disclosures can be placed and
acceptable terminology.

Alice draws upon additional resources to contextualise her identification of undi­
sclosed ads. This includes showing content shared by the influencer previously on Tik­
Tok and other platforms – sometimes disclosed as advertising – to show why this
specific instance also should have been disclosed. Regarding Emily’s Ninja Creami con­
[tent, Alice (2023a, see Supplemental data) points out Emily’s repeated use of the brand](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
name and her market value: ‘There is no way that with one million followers, Emily Can­
ham was not compensated to talk about that right then.’ These clear assertions, therefore,
identify content like Emily’s as unquestionably undisclosed commercial content that
needs to be called out.

Alongside influencers invoking professional expertise through their attention to detail,
decoding content, and gathering evidence, governance is also portrayed as personal and
[emotional. Alice (2024a, see Supplemental data) describes recognising undisclosed ads as](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
an embodied affective experience, recalling how her ‘compliance spidey senses’ felt ‘tin­
gly’ when seeing Chris’s Australian content. She feels ‘totally vindicated’ by news media
reports affirming it was of a commercial nature, but it is unclear whom she feels vindi­
cated against. This framing suggests petty drama (Geusens et al., 2023), blurring the lines
between personal relationships and accountability practices. Similarly, in one of Emily’s
(2023a) callouts, the on-screen text states: ‘When I’m in a bad mood, I go to the #bloom­
partner hashtag and report all the improperly disclosed ads,’ overlaid with a screen
recording of her reporting. Again, #partner is decoded as advertising and, therefore, is
grounds for reporting, using a private governance mechanism rather than a national
[authority, as Alice (2024c, see Supplemental data) did. It is implied that this action posi­](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
tively affected Emily’s ‘bad mood,’ alluding to a sense of justice and foregrounding
agency, while also leaning into enforcement of regulations for personal reasons and gov­
ernance as drama.

Across the callout videos, only women and queer men influencers have their disclos­
[ure practices scrutinised. Bee Better (2024a, see Supplemental data) uses Hayley’s incor­](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
rect disclosure of her Kiehl’s ad as the core example in his critique of influencers, only
also naming Mikayla Nogueira, the ‘ _queen_ of not disclosing her partnerships and her
brand affiliations,’ akin to Alice’s description of Grace as the ‘ _queen_ of illegal ad practices.’
This highlights the gendered policing of influencers (Duffy et al., 2022).

The implications of calling out specific individuals as representative is acknowledged
[by Bee Better (2024a, see Supplemental data). He pre-empts the interpretation of calling](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)

out as ‘bullying’ by positioning disclosures as part of the influencer’s professional identity
and as part of his role as governor, upholding accountability, rather than participant in
gossip or drama culture. In his follow-up video, Bee Better (2024b, see [Supplemental](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
[data) clarifies his intention was ‘not to send hate,’ nor does he see Hayley as the ‘poster](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)

as a form of peer regulation, can have serious consequences for the individual being
called out, which Bee Better hints at. Additionally, he is hesitant to accept responsibility
for how focusing on one of Hayley’s videos may impact her, raising questions about what
ethics should guide calling out practices, especially given the gendered nature of the
industry.


**Commenters support and contestation of disclosure as governance**


In callout videos, influencers present calling out undisclosed ads as necessary because
undisclosed ads are illegal, unethical, and immoral; influencers are knowingly non-com­
pliant, deceptive, and driven by commercial interests, while audiences remain vulnerable,
gullible, and discerning. Examining the ten most liked comments and their replies across
these videos, I analyse how the value of disclosure is negotiated as a form of governance.
While commenters support and expand on the narratives in callout videos, they also
challenge the premise of the callout, question the influencer’s neutrality, and discuss
the named and shamed influencer moving beyond the issue of disclosure.


_**Discerning users**_


Some of the analysed comments show how TikTok users understand regulations that
affect influencer content. Users referred to regulators and authorities, compared disclos­
ure rules across different countries, and debated the correct form of disclosure based on
legal guidelines. This indicates that viewing undisclosed content as illegal reflects an
awareness of influencers’ disclosure responsibilities. For example, comments about
‘brand ambassador’ and thanking sponsors as insufficient disclosure were points on ter­
minology that were not discussed in the videos. Thus, the inability of audiences to decode
undisclosed ads and their subsequent need for protection through public calling are sub­
tly challenged.

Comments also served as a space for users to share their experiences reporting undi­
sclosed ads to the ASA, FTC, and TikTok. Interestingly, this was mainly evident in callout
videos that did not instruct people to do so. Some users reported blocking accounts,
suggesting the aversion extends beyond the undisclosed ad of the influencer. These differ­
ent reporting mechanisms indicate how users actively respond to undisclosed ads,
demonstrating how governance systems for submitting feedback and complaints are
proactively taken up.

Comments also discussed the role of advertising in the influencer industry and on Tik­
Tok. Some users highlighted the necessity of influencer marketing as a critical way for
influencers to earn money. The TikTok algorithm was theorised to limit the visibility
of disclosed ads, so some users intentionally interacted with disclosed ads as a form of
support. Conversely, others stated they would scroll past content once they recognised
it as an ad. In a thread on Bee Better’s (2024a, see [Supplemental data) video, phrases](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)

decoded as common influencer language, especially in ad content, were discussed. These
comments gesture towards a theory of nondisclosure: when users are alerted to the com­
mercial nature of content, they disengage. Users expressed frustration over what they saw
as an infiltration of ads on TikTok, both platform ads and influencer ads, and a culture of
overpromotion by influencers, to the extent that some comments implied all influencer
content was advertising. The visibility of disclosure, while protecting consumers, also,
then, works against influencers by reminding users they are the audience commodity
being sold to advertisers.


_**Disputing the value of calling out**_


Calling out undisclosed ads as a form of governance was also the subject of comments.
Some users praised and thanked influencers for callout videos, seeing them as holding
influencers accountable and sparking necessary conversations. Comments indicating
surprise that the identified influencer content was an ad also alluded to the necessity
of calling out such content. However, the absence of disclosure was not seen as manip­
ulative. The narrative that undisclosed ads are harmful to audiences was only present
when referring to gullible ‘others’ compared to the more discerning ‘self.’ This perception
often reflected generational differences (e.g., Gen Z; Sephora kids), sometimes referen­
cing the commenter’s own children.

Other comments critiqued influencers for their callouts. Users expressed indiffer­
ence toward disclosures, claiming the issue was not that serious, deep, or meaningful,
and suggested that public criticism was not an act of governance. Rather, it was seen as
an attempt to undermine successful influencers out of jealousy, as unnecessary moral
policing, and to stir up drama. Sometimes, this involved recognising the benefit of cal­
lout videos for generating likes or indirectly promoting their businesses. In this way,
users show how they are aware of the potential monetisation of callout culture and
question the motives behind it because of how these influencers are involved in the
industry.


_**Disclosure to hate pipeline**_


There was also substantial evidence that attention placed on called-out influencers
because of their disclosure practices was redirected to criticising other aspects. This
included the influencer’s likeability, relatability, appearance, voice, and personality.
For example, comments speculated about Mikayla’s use of Ozempic shift to engage
in body-shaming and misogyny, detached from the disclosure issue, much like the
findings of Lee and Abidin (2024). Alongside negative remarks, comments also
amplified the portrayal of influencers as deceptive, fake, lying, and untrustworthy,
intensifying narratives from videos and invoking a gendered policing of authenticity
(Duffy et al., 2022). The undisclosed ad is used as a synecdoche for the influencer’s
trustworthiness. This raises questions about the limits of governance through calling
out undisclosed ads, given the blurred boundaries between viewing an influencer as
an individual and a professional actor. Although influencers who initiate the callout
in their videos do not express harmful or hateful feelings or beliefs, some users treat
it as an opportunity to surface such views through their comments.

_**Audience ambivalence towards undisclosed ads**_


The mixed feelings about disclosure seen in comments on callout videos are also evident
in comments on content identified as undisclosed ads. 24 videos had no comments con­
cerning disclosure or advertising, while out of 2342 comments on 10 videos, 211 dis­
cussed disclosure and advertising issues. This shows ambivalence toward the issue,
which is reflected in how it is expressed.

Firstly, 2 out of 478 comments on Mikayla’s video featuring Tim McGraw, identified
[by Bee Better (2024b, see Supplemental data) as an undisclosed ad, anticipated and called](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
out that this was commercial content. Both comments were standalone, received few
likes, and had no replies.

Secondly, seven out of Amelia’s 10 videos featuring the Bentley include comments that
address the lack of disclosure. Crucially, these are not stand-alone comments but are
nested within a thread. When a user asks a question or mentions the car, the number
of Bentley videos, or Amelia’s financial situation, another user identifies the car as a
‘loan’ or ‘gift,’ and that this renders the content an ‘ad,’ ‘paid promotion,’ or ‘partnership.’
Disclosure is viewed as providing essential context for understanding the content, as
users help govern commercial content when they see others being misled. Calling out
is therefore seen as a supportive action for the community.

Thirdly, the video with the most comments ( _n_ = 142) about disclosure and advertising
is on Hayley’s video advertising Kiehl’s, reposted with the ‘correct’ disclosure after the
callout. On the one hand, comments praised the repost as a sign of accountability, hon­
esty, and a gracious response to the callout, portraying her as a role model for the indus­
try. Several users mentioned their intention to buy the product and follow Hayley.
Although less common, others challenged this support, suggesting she knowingly vio­
lated rules and disclosed for the wrong reasons. Additionally, comments referenced
Bee Better’s callout, contributing to a feedback loop where Bee Better drives engagement
beyond his own content. Overall, these comments show a mostly positive outcome of the
[callout, despite Bee Better’s (2024b, see Supplemental data) reflection implying she faced](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
hate, which hints at some moderation of negative comments.

While videos contain declarative assertions that univocally establish the necessity of
publicly calling out influencers as an act of good governance, the issue becomes more
diluted when looking at the comments on the content itself. Undisclosed ads are rarely
called out in comments, even though transparency is perhaps most needed here – the site
where consumers may be most vulnerable to commercial messaging. Yet, while this
might suggest low awareness, it does not account for comment moderation, other actions
like reporting, or how audiences may decode commercial intent when disclosures are not
present.


**Discussion**


Across jurisdictions, legal advertising, consumer and media law require such commercial
content to be demarcated from so-called organic content to provide transparency to
audiences (Goanta & Ranchordás, 2020), which is reinforced by platform rules similarly
establishing disclosure as a crucial governance mechanism (Annabell et al., 2025). While
the presence of undisclosed ads on feeds has been subject to regulatory concern and


14 T. ANNABELL


scholarly attention in disciplinary contexts of assessing the effectiveness of disclosure for
consumers (Boerman, 2020; Evans et al., 2017; Pergelova & Hachey, 2023) or computa­
tionally identifying undisclosed ads (Bertaglia et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2021; Zarei et al.,
2020), I have addressed how undisclosed ads are interpreted and understood by influen­
cers and audiences.


Influencers engaged in callouts view disclosures as a necessary legal duty, ethical acts
of transparency, and a reflection of their professional identity. While some users, accord­
ing to the analysed comments, reinforce this view, others assume a commercial intent
behind content production regardless of disclosure. Within the distribution of govern­
ance in the creator economy, there is thus a convergence among some influencers and
audiences with regulators that disclosure _is_ the way to govern commercial content
with attempts to enforce rules, thereby conferring legitimacy on this formal legal frame­
work. As the European Union drafts the Digital Fairness Act, this study indicates disclos­
ures do matter. But, knowing when to disclose is treated largely as unproblematic,
smoothing out challenges about how to interpret and apply existing legal frameworks,
which regulators could help to rectify. The straightforward argument that influencers
are knowingly uncompliant is only nuanced in user comments rather than in callout
videos, demonstrating how the narrativisation of a legal obligation becomes tied to
moral and ethical assessment, and is shaped by calling out practices, which makes it dis­
tinct from monitoring of compliance. Overall, the absence of disclosure is assumed to be
deliberate, aimed at furthering the influencer’s commercial goals and hidden from certain
groups, requiring media literacy to decode its commercial nature effectively. This con­
struction relies on the audience being presented as vulnerable and gullible because of
their age, gender, and parasocial relationship, while also being expected to actively par­
ticipate in regulating commercial content by reporting. The tension between these two
positions remains resolved within callout content and indicates a need for literacies
around commercial content.


Alongside addressing how a legal framework is interpreted in the creator economy
(Hallinan et al., 2024; Kaye & Gray, 2021), I have also contributed to research on calling
out practices (Geusens et al., 2023; Lawson, 2021; Lee & Abidin, 2024; Lewis & Christin,
2022; Nakamura, 2015), which reconfigures issues of accountability as legal knowledge
becomes a source of content and is connected to the commercial position of influencers.
Conceptually, I have proposed that calling out practices reveals how influencers and
audiences position themselves as governors of the creator economy. For these individ­
uals, the identification and critique of undisclosed ads demonstrate an endorsement of
governance through disclosure, which informs their actions of calling out and reporting
to platforms or regulators as facilitating the enforcement of rules deemed necessary and
essential. Attending to both influencers and users by tracing narratives and interpret­
ations across callout videos, their comments, and comments on content believed to be
non-compliant allows the variety and intensity of callout practices among different actors
to be grappled with. This, I propose, furthers methodological approaches of calling out
practices, which previously focused either on influencers or audiences.

My findings also speak to the limits of social media callouts as a form of accountabil­
ity due to engagement and drama logics, which have implications beyond this case
study. As the analysis has demonstrated, influencers leverage their legal knowledge
by monitoring influencer activity, decoding their undisclosed commercial content,


INFORMATION, COMMUNICATION & SOCIETY 15


and publicly calling out undisclosed ads in the name of governance, which is packaged
as engaging and even rage-baiting content. Therefore, accountability is firmly
embedded in visibility strategies and monetisation practices: influencers are promoting
themselves as governors of the creator economy, commenters are fuelling drama, and
both are deciding what is worth attention. These dynamics influence the format and
tone of callout videos and create incentives for spreading regulatory discussions.
Engagement with callout content benefits the influencer, and depending on their par­
ticipation in the TikTok creator fund, may translate into income. Critically, callout
content may dovetail with additional business activities such as selling products or ser­
vices such as influencer coaching about legal obligations. This means that calling out as
a ‘discursive accountability practice’ (Clark, 2020, p. 89) is mediated by callout as con­
tent genre and source of drama (Geusens et al., 2023; Lewis & Christin, 2022) and as
such, research on calling out must be analytically sensitive towards identifying how
accountability is distributed and for what ends.

The limits of what calling out are also evident in the drift from the issue of disclosure
in callout content and comments. Because of its relationship to advertising and income,
calling out undisclosed ads by specific influencers becomes a way to dismiss influencer
labour and to justify attacking and insulting influencers, separate from the issue of dis­
closure. These attacks tend to be gender-coded and continue the pattern of gendered
moral policing of influencers (Duffy et al., 2022; Geusens et al., 2023). Against the back­
drop of feminised digital labour, the so-called unethical and immoral practice of not dis­
closing, coupled with the assumed commercial motivations of influencers, allows
(non)compliance to be another stage on which work is minimised. The gendered stereo­
type of ‘rich, vain, young women’ (Abidin, 2016) takes on additional counters of lack of
responsibility and professionalism as represented through their poor governance. This
also applies to influencers who share callout content. Lacey (2024b, see [Supplemental](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
[data) mentioned in her follow-up video that she received ‘hateful comments’ and was](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2026.2674878)
‘attack[ed] the way that I look and attack[ed] me because they’re upset that I’m holding
an influencer that they have a parasocial relationship with accountable for their actions.’
Both videos had comments disabled when the data were collected, speaking to the shift in
focus from disclosure to identity, which impacts her ongoing presence on the platform.
Thus, accountability is also seen as extending to audiences, as some influencers also
implore people to reflect on their responses to callouts, alluding to the boundaries
between calling out harmful behaviour and causing harm.

The role that TikTok plays in governing and benefiting from the creator economy,
including undisclosed ads, calling out practices and harmful responses, is largely over­
looked in calling out practices. Yet, the platform is deeply implicated, and I argue this
research has implications for areas in which they should assume greater responsibility
in the governance landscape. This could take the form of proactive identification of undi­
sclosed ads hosted on their platform and influencers could be nudged to check their dis­
closure of content flagged as potentially content. Platforms could debunk the assumption
that disclosure is algorithmically punished through the provision of data, recognising
how this theory disincentivises disclosure. In addition, platforms could reflect on how
their algorithmic systems might reward the amplification of drama or personal attacking
of influencers in the surfacing of content and comments.

**Conclusion**


This article has examined how disclosure, as a form of governance, is the subject of call­
ing out on TikTok. By analysing callout videos, their comments, and comments on the
alleged undisclosed ads, the study has addressed interpretations of disclosure as (1)
meaningful, aligning with the influencer’s commercial goals, and leaving certain groups
vulnerable because they are presumed to be unable to decode its commercial nature; and
(2) unnecessary due to ambivalence toward governance through disclosure. I have argued
there is a strategic use of regulation by actors who position themselves as governors of
influencer culture.

This study is limited, however, by its focus on a small number of TikTok videos calling
out disclosure of influencers within beauty and lifestyle content. The visibility of com­
ments used in this analysis is also shaped by moderation processes of TikTok and
influencers. As such, the understanding of what disclosure means and how it is made
to matter emerges from the particularities of TikTok cultures and governance regimes
as well as their representation within comment and content rather than being reflective
of personal beliefs. Nevertheless, their articulation on TikTok has implications for the
ongoing negotiation of governance because of the engagement with the topic in public

spaces.

Future studies could explore how calling out of disclosure across different platforms
and content niches, allowing for comparative reflections, particularly in relation to gen­
der. Conducting interviews and focus groups with influencers and audiences to examine
understanding of regulation in the creator economy more broadly, as well as the specific
mechanisms of governance through disclosure and calling-out practices, would offer
valuable qualitative insights into the intersection of regulation, influencer practices,
and consumer harms. Additionally, how _good_ governance is distributed across the eco­
system requires further theorisation and empirical research to inform regulatory discus­
sions, particularly as regulators focus on influencer marketing, as indicated by the
European Union’s Digital Fairness Act.
