Franchises remain the financial engine of the film industry - and young audiences are the
fuel. In 2025, **[more than half](https://luminatedata.com/blog/how-film-franchise-power-shifted-in-2025/)** (53%) of films made by the seven major studios were

franchises, including most of the higher-budget films. Since this trend will likely continue –

after all, 9 of the 10 highest-grossing films of 2025 were franchises – it’s worth better

understanding what makes franchises work: young audiences. Young people are often the

core demographic for franchises: they engage strongly with their fandoms long-term and

love to **[see movies during opening weekends](https://www.latimes.com/entertainment-arts/business/story/2024-10-24/ucla-report-teenagers-teens-entertainment-habit-movies)**, both of which help drive franchises’
success. And Gen Z (around age 13 to 28) were the **[most common moviegoers](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/gen-z-movie-theaters-1236526243/)** ; in 2025,

they made up **39 percent** of the North American movie audience. It’s not surprising then

that the two biggest **[domestic box office successes](https://www.boxofficemojo.com/year/2025/?grossesOption=calendarGrosses)** in 2025 were carried by young

audiences: The Minecraft Movie, an adaptation of a game **[beloved by Gen Z,](https://www.vox.com/culture/408531/minecraft-movie-memes-box-office-explained)** and Lilo &

Stitch, a nostalgic live action remake aimed at **[older Gen Z moviegoers](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-features/lilo-and-stitch-and-the-power-of-zillennial-nostalgia-1236231230/)** .


What do young audiences want from franchises? The box office data gives us one

potential answer: **diverse representation** . Adolescents today are more racially diverse

than any prior generation, more likely to identify as LGBTQ+, and more likely to have grown

up alongside peers from different backgrounds, and it shows. According to the **[UCLA](https://socialsciences.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/UCLA-Hollywood-Diversity-Report-2025-Theatrical-Film-2-27-2025.pdf)**

**[Hollywood Diversity Report](https://socialsciences.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/UCLA-Hollywood-Diversity-Report-2025-Theatrical-Film-2-27-2025.pdf)**, films with more diverse casts make more money: for
instance, films with casts that were between 40% and 50% people of color (the U.S.
population is 45.2% people of color) **made more than double their budgets on average**,

while films with casts that were at least 90% White lost money on average. This pattern of

success for more diverse casts has been consistent across **[years of data](https://socialsciences.ucla.edu/hollywood-diversity-report-2026/)** for domestic

and global box office earnings. Yet studios have been wary of backing these projects with

higher budgets: diverse representation in film is often concentrated in lower-budget, nonfranchise films. The breakdown of **[2024 films](https://socialsciences.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/UCLA-Hollywood-Diversity-Report-2025-Theatrical-Film-2-27-2025.pdf)** by the lead's identity illustrates this pattern:




Only **42.3%** of POC-led films had budgets over **$30 million**
Under half **42.9%** of White woman-led films had budgets over **$30 million**
Nearly three quarters **(71.4%)** of White man-led films had budgets over **$30 million**


In other words, many films that star a person of color or a woman are smaller-budget

films, while larger budget films, which includes most franchises, tend to have White male

leads. Studios are hesitant to fully embrace diversity in franchises, likely because they

perceive diversity as risky.


In this study, we wanted to test if asking adolescents could reduce that risk and make the
success of diverse projects more predictable. Adolescents (ages 13-24) today are not

only more diverse than any prior generation, they have also grown up in a media

environment that shows more diversity than previous generations got to see. All that

exposure builds fluency. We would expect that adolescents, despite their age, are savvy

consumers of media with representation of people of color, LGBTQ+ people, and women.

So we asked them about representation in franchises ourselves, and compared that to

franchise box office success. The real question we wanted to know: **can asking**

**adolescents about representation help studios make hit franchises?**

##### Definitions

**Adolescents** : In this report, we consider adolescents to be young people aged 13 to 24, but
we acknowledge that many neuroscientists and developmental psychologists now consider
adolescence to extend from ages 10-24. Note: In some places in the report, we use “Gen Z”
as a shorthand for adolescents, but there is a small difference between the two groups;
when the data were collected, Gen Z was considered to be people aged 13 to 28.


**Authentic Inclusive Representation:** For participants, we defined this as, “Authentically
inclusive representation of [identity group] people means portraying both [identity group]
individuals and their culture prominently in the movie, in a way that is genuine and
respectful. This means a [identity group] viewer would feel understood by the movie, seeing
themselves and their culture accurately represented.”


**Diverse Representation:** When we use this term in this report, we are referring to
representation of people with marginalized identities, including people of color, women, and
LGBTQ+ individuals.


**Lived Experience:** Living with an identity (e.g. Latino/a/e or LGBTQ+ identities) and gaining
knowledge of and expertise around that identity through personal experience rather than
exclusively through learning about the identity from other people.


**Film Franchises:** Films that have other media properties within the same IP, including
multiple films. For example, the franchises in our dataset were: Avatar, Barbie, Black Panther,
Blue Beetle, Bob's Burgers, Captain Marvel, Coco, Crazy Rich Asians, Creed, Doctor Strange,
Downton Abbey, Fantastic Beasts, Fast & Furious, Frozen, Incredibles, Knives Out, Minions,
Moana, Ocean's Eight, Spider-Verse, Strange World, The Batman, The Color Purple, The
Hunger Games, The Jungle Book, Thor, Tyler Perry's Madea, Ultraman: Rising, Wreck-It Ralph.

Grounding characterization in authentic relationships


Adolescents who said that they connected especially strongly with a character because the

### CONCLUSION

The findings in this report make a simple but powerful case: adolescents are not just a

core audience for franchise films — they are sophisticated judges of their diverse

representation. They notice when a culture is rendered with depth and care, when a

character is given a meaningful story rather than a tokenizing cameo, and when the people

on screen feel like actual human beings with relationships worth caring about. And when

they find representation that meets that bar, they show up — at the box office, in

merchandise sales, and in the fandoms that sustain a franchise for the long term.


The throughline at the heart of these findings is something studios already know but don’t

always act on: authenticity is an asset, not a constraint. Adolescents with lived experience
(those judging representation of their own identity) carry an expertise that develops early

- 13-year-olds already have it. That means elevating teen perspectives in the

development process; if they share the identity being represented, their read on

authenticity is highly predictive of box office success, arguably even more than the

expert consultants studios typically rely on. The three fatal flaws adolescents identified
(a lack of cultural grounding, minimal roles for diverse characters, and characters who
don't feel relatable) are all correctable. And there is a clear path forward: center real,

culturally-embedded relationships. When diverse characters have meaningful bonds with

one another that bring out their identities, it lays the groundwork for representation that

even adolescents consider authentic. Authentic representation drives stronger

connections to the franchise, and that connection drives success.


The franchises adolescents rated most highly (and that performed best at the box
office) didn't just feature diverse characters. They built worlds around them that included

people they loved and authentic cultural elements, and let all of those things help inform

the story.


At CSS, our guiding principle is the same one that produced these findings: ask young

people good questions and take their answers seriously. For storytellers and executives
looking to build franchises that last, we're here to help bridge that gap, because when the

industry listens to adolescents, everyone wins.

### CONCLUSION

For studios who want to act on these findings, here are **four** **concrete tips** :


# **1** **2** **3** **4**



**Consult young people during production.** Adolescents, especially
those with lived experience of the identity being represented, are
strong judges of authentic representation that will resonate, and their
demographic is key to franchise success.


**Be wary of the three fatal flaws.** During writing and production, ask
whether the representation in your film has genuine cultural grounding,
whether diverse characters have meaningful roles, and whether

audiences have the chance to relate to those characters.


**Don't be afraid to make relationships the center of the story.**
Relationships between friends, family members, and even romantic
relationships can help illustrate the specificity of culture and identity
that mark great representation.


**Build adolescent representation assessment into testing.** Adding an
explicit measure of perceived authentic representation, especially
when your sample includes adolescents, could help predict success
for your film and any future installments.








### METHOD

Project Description:


The purpose of this study was to test whether adolescents are an important and potentially
overlooked authority on diverse representation in modern film, especially for franchises whose
success depends on young people.


To conduct this study, CSS partnered with global insights and strategy firm, National Research
Group (NRG), to survey 12,180 adolescents aged 13-24 via syndicated tracking. Franchise IQ is
NRG's always-on syndicated tracker focused on entertainment franchises. It is in the field every
week in the US, interviewing 3,500 general entertainment consumers per week. Semi-annually
fielding 5,000 consumers internationally, FIQ has conducted over 1.3 million interviews globally
with measurement on nearly 1,300 franchises. These franchises have touchpoints across key
verticals including toys, gaming, streaming, television, and theatrical. Each franchise, including
those featured in this report, is defined holistically - when respondents are asked about
Spider-Man, for example, they are answering about their feelings toward the Spider-Man
franchise as whole, encompassing its comics, movies, television series, etc.


Utilizing NRG's Franchise IQ, we asked adolescent respondents who were familiar with any of 29
popular franchises to rate the representation of a specific identity group portrayed in that

franchise. To select the 29 franchises, we had four criteria: first, the franchise had to be

currently tested by our data collection partner NRG; second, the franchise had to be
represented in our AIR ratings so that we could make comparisons between AIR ratings and
adolescents’ ratings, third, the franchise had to include a film that was released in the past ten
years, and fourth, no R-rated movies given the younger participants in the study. These criteria
narrowed it down to 29 franchises and ratings of representation of six identities: Black,
Hispanic/Latinx, Asian American-Pacific Islander, Indigenous/Native, Women, and LGBTQ+. For
seven of the franchises, we had expert ratings of multiple identities, so there were a total of 38

rated identities.


We asked about racial, gender, and LGBTQ+ representation - specifically, portrayals of Black,
Hispanic/Latinx, Asian American-Pacific Islander, Indigenous/Native, Women, and LGBTQ+
people. For adolescents who were familiar with the franchise, we asked them to rate the type(s)
of representation portrayed in that franchise on a 5-point scale using two questions:

**1.** “How much does this franchise represent [identity group] culture or [identity group] people in
a good or positive, realistic, or three-dimensional way?”


18


**2.** “ _‘Authentically inclusive representation’ of [identity group] people means portraying_
_both [identity group] individuals and their culture prominently in the movie, in a way that is_
_genuine and respectful. This means a [identity group] viewer would feel understood by the_
_movie, seeing themselves and their culture accurately represented._ Overall, how well did the
movie include authentically inclusive representation of [identity group] people that felt real
and complex?


We calculated each participant’s AIR Rating using the average of their scores on these two
questions. To get a clearer picture of why they rated the franchise the way they did, we asked
three follow-up questions:

“How much does this franchise include stereotypes about [identity group] people that
some might consider problematic or harmful?”
“Which character do you relate to the most?” “Why?” [Six options: The character’s
relationship(s), personality trait(s), story arc, likability, uniqueness, or shared identity]
“Choose the option that best describes the biggest flaw with the representation of [identity
group] people:” [Six options: The identity group’s minimal role, stereotypical characteristics,
lack of real or relatable characters, problematic or unrealistic relationships, inauthentic story
arc, or lack of authentic cultural representation]

Participants:

Our final sample included 23,284 participants ranging in age from 13 to 24 years old.
Participants ranged broadly across this age span, with representation from each age cohort
between 13 and 24. Each age category had a minimum of 1,017 participants in it. Additionally,
each franchise had at least 485 participants who rated it. The racial composition of the sample
included respondents identifying as White (54.4%), Hispanic and/or Latino (20.9%), Black or
African American (15.8%), Native American or Indigenous (4.5%), Asian or Asian American (2.5%),
and Middle Eastern or North African (1.0%), with smaller proportions identifying as another race
