# **Diminishing or increasing? Exploring the impact of online** **movie reviews on consumer demand within and across two** **sequential release stages**

**Yang Wang** **[1]** **· Kangkang Qi** **[2]** **· Pei Xu** **[2]**

**Abstract**

This study examines the impact of online movie reviews on consumer demand within
and across two sequential release stages: theatrical run and online streaming. In contrast to extant findings of a diminishing sales effect of online reviews, we observe
an increasingly positive impact of movie reviews in the cinema release stage, in line
with the theory of diffusion of innovations. Moreover, consumers are predominantly
conservative in using product reviews to select foreign products due to the cultural
gap. After movies are released online, the role of product reviews changes according to the release gap. Movies with a higher review valence and volume during the
cinema release will continue to benefit from the reviews with a shorter release gap. In
contrast, movies that receive a lower review valence in the early market benefit from
setting a longer release gap and waiting for the online buzz to dissipate.

**1 Introduction**


Business researchers have extensively studied the sales impact of online product
reviews–a type of word of mouth (WOM) (e.g., Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Liu,
2006
). Whereas offline WOM is usually perishable over time (Elberse & Eliashberg, 2003), online reviews are documented on websites. Thus, potential buyers
can easily access them at any time (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006), meaning they
can see previously accumulated product evaluations. In line with this logic, the
sales impact of online WOM should be persistent, if not increasing, over time.

However, extant studies analyzing the temporal role of online reviews reveal
a diminishing sales effect, such that their impact on consumer demand decreases
over time (e.g., Cui et al., 2012; Hu et al., 2008; Liu, 2006). The authors attribute this result to consumers’ reduced product interest in the late periods of the
product life cycle (PLC) – proxied as the amount of time since release. Perhaps
for this reason, many sellers focus on gathering positive reviews during the early
period of a product release, even if the reviews may not be truthful (He et al.,
2022). Yet, such a strategy may depreciate the value of online reviews, leading
consumers to make less informed purchase decisions.

_Do online product reviews indeed have a decreasing impact on sales?_ We take
two perspectives to address this question. The theory of diffusion of innovations
(Rogers, 1962) characterizes early consumer groups (i.e., innovators and early
adopters) as less risk-averse and often opinion leaders in the sense that they share
their evaluations to influence future buyers. By contrast, late consumer groups
(i.e., the majorities and laggards) are more conservative and tend to rely heavily on early consumer groups’ opinions. They may only adopt a product after it
receives enough positive WOM. Accordingly, online consumer opinions – usually
cumulated and saved on the review platforms – should have an increasing (rather
than diminishing) effect on sales throughout the PLC.

From a data analytics perspective, we see two challenges regarding the temporal role of product reviews that extant studies have not addressed. First, observing
the entire life cycle of many products is difficult. For example, a book can be on
the market for decades; in this case, estimations using a sample with an arbitrarily
truncated observation window may lead to biased results. Second, when data on the
complete life cycles of products are available, researchers often get an imbalanced
panel because products with poor sales performance usually quit the market early.
Separating the data by time (e.g., week) and applying an ordinary least squares
(OLS) model (e.g., Moon et al., 2010) may produce less significant estimates in
the late periods due to the reduced variations in the review measures since most
remaining products are popular on the market and thus have similar review features
(e.g., the variance of average rating is limited). Taking these factors into account,
the findings of a decreasing sales effect of reviews may be inconclusive.

To overcome the data analysis challenges, we employ several rigorous
approaches to understand the relationship between product reviews and consumer
demand over time. First, similar to Liu (2006), we conduct research in the context of the motion picture industry. Movies have a short cinema release window;

generating (positive) reviews during the early period cannot improve sales in the
long run; _only_ products that continue to receive positive reviews can do so.

However, late consumer groups are much more conservative when choosing American movies than when choosing their Chinese counterparts. The sheer increase in
the average rating cannot improve sales. Instead, consumers only consider movies
with high levels of _both_ review valence and volume, and such an interaction effect
increases over time. American movies are produced in a culture unfamiliar to many
late adopters in China. They may not have enough cultural background and knowledge to appreciate such products unless they receive substantial positive comments,
which indicate that the movies are acceptable to most audiences in their culture.
Overall, these findings further support our proposed conjecture that the impact of
online reviews on consumer demand increases over time.

**2.2 Foreign products and cultural gap**


Another topic we cover is the differential role of reviews for products produced in a
familiar culture versus foreign products. Specifically, in the context of the Chinese
market, we compare the effect of reviews on consumer demand between domestically produced movies and American movies. To Chinese consumers, American
movies are produced in a foreign culture, which is likely to have greater product
uncertainty in contrast to domestically produced movies.

A cultural gap usually occurs when a disconnection is evident between the home
and foreign markets (Hoskins & Mirus, 1998). Consumers buying products produced in other cultures may experience higher uncertainty (Safari et al., 2013). They
need more information to appreciate a foreign product due to their lack of cultural
background (Lee, 2006). For example, Gao et al. (2020) reveal that the informativeness of the movie title translation (which helps consumers understand the story) significantly improves a Hollywood movie’s Chinese box office revenue.

Online product reviews should be another valuable information source to reduce
such uncertainty stemming from cultural gaps. A few research works have compared
consumers’ review generation behaviors between cultures (e.g., Fang et al., 2013;
Koh et al., 2010). Yet, to the best of our knowledge, little extant research analyzes
how movie reviews affect consumers in selecting foreign products. The estimations
in our study address this question.


**2.3 Cross‑platform release strategy**


The last stream of research related to our study is the cross-platform release strategy,
a popular topic in the marketing field. While Calzada and Valletti (2012) suggest
the benefit of a shorter release gap, their models allow consumers to make repeat
purchases; that is, they can watch movies both in the theater and online. Similarly,
August et al. (2015) demonstrate that a shorter release gap can increase profits for
high-quality movies. Luan and Sudhir (2022) identify a trade-off between the negative impact of a short gap and the dissipation of positive WOM as the gap increases.
All of these studies mainly employ analytical modeling approaches.

Our empirical probe focuses on first-time buyers and accounts for the impact

product reviews should shorten the release gap if they want to benefit from WOM

**3 Hypotheses development**


We develop the hypotheses for the temporal role of online product reviews in terms
of three dimensions: their impact on consumer demand during the first (cinema
release) stage, their impact on foreign cultural products in contrast to domestically
produced products, and their effect on consumer demand during the late (online)
stage.


**3.1 The Role of product reviews within a product life cycle**


Product life cycle (PLC) refers to the chronological stages of a product (i.e., introduction, growth, maturity, and decline) based on its sales and the movement of consumer groups (Levitt, 1965). The theory of diffusion of innovations (Rogers, 1962)
provides a framework to explain further how consumer groups change during a
product’s life cycle. The term “innovation” in this theory can refer to any object perceived as new to consumers, including newly released products.

Throughout the diffusion process, five consumer groups sequentially adopt a
product: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards. The
first two groups tend to be less risk-averse. Innovators are especially risk-tolerant
and may adopt a new product even if there is insufficient information available about
its performance. In addition, both innovators and early adopters have strong opinion
leadership. They are inclined to give suggestions to influence future consumers. By
contrast, the remaining three consumer groups (i.e., early and late majorities and
laggards) are more conservative and skeptical during their purchase decision-making process. They seldom play the role of opinion leaders and tend to rely on early
consumers’ WOM. Although the consumer groups have no clear boundaries, they
often appear chronologically, overlapping with a product’s sales growth in its life
cycle.

Because online reviews are often accumulated and recorded on websites, prospective consumers can readily find review information on a given website. In
line with the characteristics of the first two consumer groups, we should observe
that the sales impact of product reviews at an early period of product release is
limited because these buyers are more risk-tolerant and do not necessarily need
to rely on others’ opinions. Note that although the level of product uncertainty at
the early period of a PLC is very high (probably due to the lack of online WOM),
innovators and early adopters may evaluate a product using other information,


Marketing Letters (2025) 36:963–1001 973


e.g., existing product features. In our research setting, for example, early consumer groups may include fans of a movie star or lovers of a sci-fi series. Their
deep interest in the product or its related characteristics may offset the uncertainty about the product or help them formulate a positive bias toward the product (Li & Hitt, 2008). As a result, they do not consult product reviews prior to
making a purchase decision. By contrast, late consumer groups do not have such
a strong tie to the product and tend to be more risk-averse, according to the theory of diffusion of innovation. They may not be persuaded to adopt a product
unless it gathers enough consumer endorsements (i.e., positive WOM). As the
PLC evolves, the later the consumer group, the more likely they are to refer to the
existing buyers’ opinions to make purchases. Hence, we should observe that the
impact of product reviews on consumer demand increases throughout the PLC.

Our study aims to examine the sales impact of two review measures: review
valence (i.e., the average rating) and review volume (i.e., the total number of
reviews). Because most review platforms display and even highlight these two
summarized statistics on the product page, consumers may use these two signals
to decide whether to purchase the product. We formulate the hypotheses regarding these two dimensions of product reviews.

The average rating is a commonly used review feature in the literature, and
several studies show evidence of it positively affecting sales (e.g., Chintagunta
et al., 2010; Zhu & Zhang, 2010). From the consumers’ perspective, such a statistic represents the overall valence of extant buyers’ opinions of the product. A
positive overall rating suggests that most existing buyers who leave a review have
had an enjoyable product experience. Thus, it should encourage future buyers to
adopt the product. Note that even if the volume of reviews is not large, a high
valence may still indicate that the product is favored by a specific group of consumers, i.e., a niche product. For example, documentary movies are usually not a
very popular genre (in contrast to, e.g., comedy, drama) and thus may not receive
many reviews. However, a positively rated documentary movie can still be recognized as having good quality and thus attract moviegoers interested in this genre.
Because late consumer groups are more reliant on the opinions of early buyers,
we expect that the positive sales impact of review valence will increase over time.
Formally:


**H1a:** During the cinema release stage, the impact of review valence on consumer
demand increases over time.


Consumers also observe the total number of reviews on a typical product review
website. Yet, such a statistic may not have a clear relationship with sales. Although
review volume may indicate product awareness (Liu, 2006), a large number of
reviewers criticizing the product may discourage future buyers from purchasing the
product. Furthermore, when a product’s sales growth reaches its peak and starts to
decline, the cumulated review volume may be negatively tied to sales (Cui et al.,
2012
). Hence, we do not hypothesize a specific effect of review volume on consumer demand.


974 Marketing Letters (2025) 36:963–1001


A higher review volume will become more meaningful if it is considered in relation to the valence of the opinions. In particular, a positive valence with a large number of reviews indicates that the product not only has good WOM but is also widely
accepted by existing buyers. As such, in addition to the sales impact of review
valence, the interaction between review valence and volume should have a positive
effect on sales. Since consumer groups in late periods of a PLC tend to be more
conservative and risk-averse, they may put greater weight on this signal to make purchases as well. This may be especially the case when consumers have a higher level
of product uncertainty when evaluating foreign products, a point we will discuss in
the ensuing section. In line with these arguments, we expect an increasingly positive
effect of the interaction between review valence and volume on consumer demand
over time.


**H1b:** During the cinema release stage, the impact of the interaction between
review valence and review volume on consumer demand increases over time.


**3.2 Domestically produced versus foreign cultural products**


When conducting a pre-purchase evaluation of a foreign product, online product
reviews may be a more crucial resource for potential consumers. A cultural gap–a
disconnection between the home and foreign markets (Hoskins & Mirus, 1998)
– may occur in this scenario. Buyers of products produced in other cultures may
experience greater uncertainty (Safari et al., 2013) due to the lack of cultural background and knowledge to understand foreign products (Lee, 2006). Such uncertainty
could have a notably negative impact on sales in Asian countries, where uncertainty
avoidance is strong (Pratesi et al., 2021). In this situation, WOM from other buyers
may help mitigate the concerns.

To a typical Chinese moviegoer, an American movie is produced in a distant culture. It is difficult for the Chinese audience to understand the language, the punchlines, the general setting of the story, and so on. According to Rogers (1962), innovators and early adopters usually have a higher social status and education. In our
research setting, they are likely to be those familiar with American culture, including individuals with a college degree (English is a compulsory subject in Chinese
universities) and those who have studied abroad in English-speaking countries,
especially in the US. Similar to the early consumer groups of domestic movies,
these individuals are less likely to be affected by product reviews since they have an
understanding of (or even a preference for) foreign cultural products.

By contrast, those in the late periods will likely have insufficient knowledge to
appreciate these products. Unlike domestically produced movies, early consumers’
opinions about American movies may not be successfully accepted by late adopters
if these reviews have a high valence but insufficient volume. Popular social topics
in American culture, such as feminism (e.g., Down et al., 2020), race issues (e.g.,
Weaver, 2011), and democracy (e.g., Settich, 2013), which are often reflected in
movies, are not typically part of Chinese cultural ideology. Even if the early consumer groups can understand these topics (since they tend to be familiar with the

**3.3 The change in the role of product reviews across stages**


After a movie’s theatrical run, it is usually released on video streaming platforms.
This unique feature of movies creates a challenge when comparing the role of online
reviews across the two stages: whether we should treat the two stages as separate life
cycles or a single one, and depending on the answer, different expectations of the
role of product reviews in the second market (stage) will arise. We find arguments
from both sides.


On the one hand, the earliest consumers during the online stage are not necessarily innovators or early adopters because the movie is no longer a brand-new product. As such, they may not have the characteristics of the early consumer groups
during the cinema release stage. Considering that they prefer to wait to watch the
movie online at a lower cost (a point we will discuss subsequently), these moviegoers may be more conservative than the late adopters in the theatrical run. In other
words, although the product is released on a different platform in the second stage,
its sales growth can be regarded as a continuous process from the previous stage, as
it is exposed to the next consumer group. Second, these consumers may follow the
movie’s WOM during its theatrical run. By the time a movie is released online, it


976 Marketing Letters (2025) 36:963–1001


should have garnered enough reviews to indicate the “true quality” of the product.
Accordingly, the impact of online reviews on consumer demand might be even more
exaggerated in the online stage.

On the other hand, compared with moviegoers in the cinema, online users’ cost of
watching an unfavorable movie is lower. They only need to make a monthly payment
to a video streaming platform to get access to all movies on the site, and such fees
are often lower than a single movie ticket. In our research setting, a typical monthly
video streaming platform subscription costs users 15 Chinese yuan (CNY). By contrast, the average ticket price of a single movie being screened in the cinema is about
33 CNY, more than double the monthly payment. [3] Moreover, unlike the cinema
viewing experience, online users can stop video streaming at any time if they do not
enjoy the movie. With a lower consumption cost, consumers in the second stage may
be more tolerant of movies with a lower review valence. Besides, consumers in the
online stage make choices among a vast number of movies, in contrast to the limited
number of movies screened in the cinema. Given these various factors, consumers’
decisions may be less likely to be affected by reviews at this stage. Besides using
reviews, they may adopt more heuristic rules (Payne et al., 1993), such as choosing
movies based on their genre preference and relying on recommendations made by
video streaming platforms. In line with these logical inferences, we expect a less
significant effect of reviews on consumer demand.

Overall, the impact of product reviews on consumer demand after a movie’s
online release depends on the characteristics of consumers across stages – specifically, whether the later stage builds upon the earlier one or if they differ qualitatively. As such, we propose two alternative hypotheses.


**H3a:** The impact of online product reviews on consumer demand becomes
stronger in the online stage if it is a continuous process of the cinema release

stage.
**H3b:** The impact of online product reviews on consumer demand becomes less
significant in the online stage if it is not a continuous process of the cinema
release stage, i.e., if consumers adopt different product selection criteria.

**6.2 Change of consumer groups due to longer release gaps**


Our second exploration is based on the gap between the two release stages. We use
the difference between the last box office report date and the online release date to

movies have a much longer release gap (mean = 38.88 days) than their Chinese counterparts (mean = 8.68 days). More than 84.78% of Chinese movies in our data (i.e.,
39 out of 47) were released within 30 days after the movie was out of the theater,
whereas this number for American movies is only 41.18%.

We conjecture that the release gap is the potential driver for the different findings
in Sect. 5.2. When a movie is released shortly after (or even before the end of) its
theatrical run, the product remains available across stages. In this case, those planning to watch the movie online during the first stage will probably be the next consumer group. Because the movie was barely out of the theater, its online buzz is still
evolving, and consumers’ impressions of the products are affected by the continuous WOM (Luan & Sudhir, 2022). As such, the two stages may not be qualitatively

**7.1 Business implications**


The empirical findings offer several business insights. First, many sellers focus on soliciting positive reviews during the early period of a product release because they believe
this is the most critical period when online WOM affects sales. Our findings discourage such business practices. Only products that receive persistent positive reviews can
maintain a high consumer demand. Although soliciting positive reviews during the early
period of product release may help improve sales, the product sales will drop quickly
if more negative reviews surface in the late period, which may even shorten the product’s life cycle. Thus, we suggest sellers focus on improving the product quality so that
consumer groups can favor it over time. Second, the results about the consumers’ more
restrictive selection criteria for products produced in a different culture imply a more
competitive environment for foreign products. A cultural product that cannot survive in
its home market (e.g., a Hollywood flop) is unlikely to be successful in a foreign market.

Third, we suggest that sellers of products that receive good WOM in an early
market shorten the release gap between the two stages. In this way, the products
can continuously benefit from the online buzz while consumer interest remains.
Conversely, sellers of products with low review valence may consider a longer
cross-stage release gap. Such a strategy may help them avoid the negative impact
of review valence on consumer demand in the following market. In this case, those
with a higher review volume might benefit more from the WOM in the second stage.


**7.2 Limitations and future directions**


Our study suffers from a few limitations. First, we acknowledge that estimations using user-reported viewership at the online stage may not be accurate if the


Marketing Letters (2025) 36:963–1001 999


outcome does not fully represent the actual demand on the video streaming platforms. Second, although we include many factors, such as Baidu Trends and the
number of competing movies, to account for potential factors that may affect both
product review measures and the dependent variables, we are unable to address the
concern of endogeneity completely. Unlike the setting in Chintagunta et al. (2010),
where products are likely to be released sequentially across local markets, all movies in our data are released across the nation on the same date in China. As a result,
finding instrumental variables using review measures in the previous market is not
an option. We are cautious that the empirical findings in this study do not suggest
causal effects.

Lastly, we are unable to measure repeat consumption in our study. A true lover
of a movie may watch it several times. Our estimations of the impact of product
reviews on consumer demand do not account for this scenario. We encourage future
studies to address the related questions. Despite these limitations, we believe this
study sheds light on the temporal role of product reviews from novel perspectives,
i.e., across markets and over time.
