### **Abstract**

This thesis examines how online movie reviewing can transform consumption experiences.
While existing marketing research has primarily treated movie reviews as predictive outputs for
box office performance, little is known about the experiential effects of reviewing on consumers
themselves. A multi-level model of a reviewing practice (Figure 1) is created using Consumer
Culture Theory (CCT) and the Associative-Propositional Evaluation (APE) model.


Empirical materials were collected through netnography on Letterboxd and semi-structured
interviews with 13 active users, resulting in 260 user reviews and approximately 16 hours of

interview data.


Thematic analysis identified three transformational outcomes. First, online reviewing prolongs a
movie consumption experience through affective and cognitive pathways. Second, reviews can
function as temporal anchors that make episodic traces visible and revisitable. Third, online
reviewing necessitates the performance of movie consumer identities, which actively changes
consumer ideologies and the marketplace culture. The findings demonstrate that an online movie
reviewing practice affects consumption experiences.


This thesis contributes a consumer-centric perspective to movie marketing research, by
reorienting attention from financial outcomes to experiential transformations,


**Keywords: online reviewing, art consumption, movie marketing, CCT, APE model,**

**netnography, Letterboxd**

## Introduction

How do consumers understand art consumption experiences? An art consumption experience, as
defined by Holbrook & Hirschman (1982) and Lemon & Verhoef (2016), is an extended,
multi-phase process, which encompasses anticipation, live experience and reflection. This means
that an art consumption experience does not end after visiting a museum or seeing a movie in a
cinema. The consumption of art continues after the initial viewing, as opinions form, personal
meanings emerge, and memories are reconstructed (Nigatu et al., 2024). Art is then consumed
not with one singular encounter, but rather through an ongoing process of interpretation and
re-interpretation.


To provide a glimpse into how art consumption experiences are being understood, Schneider et
al., (2021) refer to art (re-)interpretations as a collection of evaluations. Schneider et al. (2021)
do also provide the caveat that researching written evaluations only reveal the outcome of the
interpretive process but not the actual process itself. Hence, it is helpful to engage with Arnould
and Thompson’s (2005) CCT framework, which provides possible reasoning as to how cultural
aspects shape the interpretive evaluation process of art consumption experiences. The need to
_review_ consumer evaluations is perhaps expressed most clearly in this quote by Gawronski
(2007, p. 579):


_“Evaluative responses play a significant role—if not the most significant role—for understanding_

_social behavior.”_


Examining the practice of online movie reviewing is well suited for uncovering art consumption
experiences, because of the multitude of possible evaluative outcomes related to film
consumption. From the plethora of subjects that written consumer evaluations can cover, movies

are both works of art and entertainment. This means that movie reviews consist of multifaceted

evaluations, ranging “from a spontaneous sense of liking or disliking to well-founded critique”
(Schneider et al., 2021, p. 584). Furthermore, when movie evaluations are expressed online they
impact other consumers (Chintagunta et al., 2010), which shows how online reviewing is a
constitutive part of CCT.


Letterboxd was selected as the thesis’ research site since it has an established movie review

culture, and a vibrant user base. The New York Times Magazine characterizes Letterboxd as "a
cinephilic hive buzzing with authentic enthusiasm and heterogeneous tastes" (Kleeman, 2026).
The user base consists of independent cinema enthusiasts, making it a concentrated site of
consumer culture and diverse opinions. Letterboxd users are prompted/encouraged to create
text-based reviews, the lengths of which range from "ultrashort, off-the-cuff takes to gonzo
film-theory-inflected texts” (Kleeman, 2026). This is distinct from other platforms like Rotten


3


Tomatoes and IMDb, where ratings are often the primary focus. However, this focus on
Letterboxd delimits the thesis in several respects, as there is no cross-platform or cross-art form
comparison.


While there is previous marketing research on movie reviewing, it has primarily focused on how
online reviews affect box office outcomes, using quantified review data to predict revenues.
Movie marketing studies have tried to explain either how expert reviews affect the decision to
see motion pictures in movie theatres (e.g., Thrane, 2018) or more generally how the movie box
office performance is correlated with online user scores (e.g., Chintagunta et al., 2010). These
results provide an understanding as to why movie reviews are an important component of digital
marketing (Moon et al., 2010). However, the conducted research has positioned movie reviews
as outcomes/predictors, meaning that reviews are aggregated into data points, which are counted,
averaged, and correlated with financial performance. This focus has left the reviewing process
itself unexplored with no discernible marketing research on the transformational effects of
reviewing on movie consumers themselves. This reveals a qualitative research gap of the existing
marketing research, leaving the question “What are the effects of reviewing on the movie
consumption experience itself?” unanswered.


The aforementioned research gap is significant for two reasons. First, it remains unclear what
features consumers are considering during evaluation and how overall evaluative judgments are
formed (Schneider et al., 2021). As Schneider et. al (2021) have already established, it is the
considered features, and how consumers process those features, that substantially affect
evaluative outcomes. This is especially evident on online movie reviewing platforms (e.g.,
Letterboxd, iMDb), where consumer reviews are often highly polarized (Nishijima et al., 2022).
Secondly, by focusing only on the financial implications of movie reviewing, a crucial part of the
consumption “backstory” is neglected. Therefore, to bridge this research gap, this thesis situates
the interpretive/evaluative processes of online movie reviewing as a constitutive part of
consumer culture, and examines the intricacies of art evaluations.


This thesis aims to reorient the extensive research on art marketing and revenue generation, by
reorienting our research focus towards the review process itself. By collecting empirical material
in the form of Letterboxd user reviews and interviews, we aim to answer how the act of

reviewing transforms the reviewer's movie consumption experience. The central research
question, based on the aims of this thesis, is as follows:


_How does the practice of online reviewing affect movie consumption experiences?_


By answering the research question, we aim to contribute to the practice of art marketing and the
connected research streams. We attempt to reorient focus from financial outcomes to experiential
outcomes in efforts to provide understanding of contemporary art consumption. Further


4


understanding the relation of online reviewing to art consumption experiences is also practically
relevant to online review platforms, marketers, and cultural institutions.

## **CCT as a Guiding Theoretical Framework for Movie** **Reviewing**


This theoretical chapter explains how the process of reviewing a movie is inherently tied with
CCT. While Practice Theory seems like an obvious fit for studying the practice of movie
reviewing, we instead use CCT as our macro-level theoretical basis. CCT is best suited since we
study the transformational effects of reviewing and not routinized doings, materials, and
competences that constitute Practice Theory (Warde, 2005). Thompson (2024, p. 555) articulates
that CCT provides an “ontological bridge” which is necessary to bridge the gap between

‑
psychological decision making processes and consumers’ co constituting relations to the
socio-material world. Hence, this thesis combines CCT with theories from psychology research
on evaluation (described further in the following APE model chapter) to enable our analysis on
how reviewing practices affect movie consumption experiences.

### **Why CCT Is Relevant to Studying Movie Reviewing**


The application of CCT perspectives to movie reviewing enables this thesis to study how
consumers construct identities, negotiate meanings, and participate in a consumption community.
It is important to clarify that CCT does not refer to one specific theory but rather to "a
heteroglossic (cf. Bakhtin, 1981), and a conceptually dynamic field of inquiry that encompasses
a multiplicity of theoretical approaches, methodological orientations, representational practices,
and which speaks to a range of stakeholders" (Arnould & Thompson, 2015, p. 4). Nevertheless,
at the core of CCT is the relationships “between lived culture and social resources,” which “are
mediated through markets" (Arnould & Thompson, 2015, p. 14). This makes CCT particularly
applicable for this study because it allows us to view movie consumption not as a singular,
individual act of purchase or evaluation, but as a part of a culturally situated process of
meaning-making (Belk, 1987; Holbrook & Hirschman, 1982).


**CCT’s Distributed View of Cultural Meaning**


CCT views culture as a non-homogenous entity, which Hannerz (1993, p. 16) calls a "distributed
view of cultural meaning." Instead of viewing culture as one system of shared meanings and
values, CCT explores how market-capitalism induced global socio-historical factors create
consumer meanings (Arnould & Thompson, 2005). CCT emphasizes on the hybridization of
consumption traditions and ways of life (Firat & Venkatesh, 1995). Although a distributive view
of culture is not unique only to CCT, this stream of marketing research is helpful for


5


understanding how consumer culture is created. Consumer culture is situated in specific
marketplace systems, and is being constantly shaped by cultural narratives (Arnould &
Thompson, 2005). For this thesis, a distributed view of cultural meaning necessarily constitutes
that there is no homogenous culture of movie reviewers. Therefore, only overlapping cultural
groupings can be observed (e.g., fans of particular genres, directors, or eras), each of which are
filled with unique meanings and practices.


**CCT Conceptualizing Culture as the Fabric of Experience**


CCT researchers view culture as the very basis of consumer experiences, and hence also
meaning, and action. Consumer culture is a framework, which constitutes consumers' options of
conceivable action, feeling, and thought, wherein certain patterns of behavior and sense-making
are likelier than others (Schmitt et al., 2022). In the case of movie reviewing, this means that a
specific platform (Letterboxd), and its specific parameters, frame what kinds of responses are
conceivable and valued. CCT positions movie consumers/reviewers as continuous “learners,”
constantly evaluating what counts as a "good" review. Reviewing practices are hence linked with
existing cultural norms, which may also be innovated upon or resisted entirely by art consumers.


**CCT Framing the Marketplace as a Cultural Resource**


Another key premise of CCT is that consumers use the marketplace as a source of symbolic
resources through which narratives of identity are constructed (Arnould & Thompson, 2005).
Consumers are "identity seekers and makers," (Arnould & Thompson, 2005, p. 871) who are
actively looking for personally meaningful symbols embedded within the marketplace to realize
self-identity. Consuming movies, the practice of reviewing and the online reviewing platform
itself are forms of symbolic resources, which make up the “diversified array of resources”
(Arnould & Thompson, 2015, p. 6) consumers can use to perform identities. Online movie
reviewers are also identity seekers, as well as meaning makers, who construct and perform their
(movie) consumer identities.

### **The Research Programs within CCT and Their Relation to Art Reviewing**


_This thesis focuses specifically on the following three research programs within CCT: Consumer_
_Identity Projects, Marketplace Cultures and Consumer Ideology._


**Consumer Identity Projects**


Of the four interrelated research domains existing within CCT research, three are of particular
relevance to this study, the first of which is Consumer Identity Projects. As the name suggests,
studies of Consumer Identity Projects investigate consumers’ sense of selves, or more
specifically, how the co-constitutive/co-productive ways of consumers, together with
marketer-generated materials, forge identities (Arnould & Thompson, 2005). Consumer identity


6


projects are most commonly considered to be goal-driven, but are often not clearly stated. Hence,
consumer goals are not inherently coherent as they can be filled with internal contradictions
(Arnould & Thompson, 2005).


This stream of research aligns with this thesis’ intent to examine how online reviewers construct
and perform identities through reviewing. As Schau & Gilly (2003) demonstrated in their study
about personal websites, consumers use commercial symbols to create digital
self-representations. Online movie reviewers also craft _reviewer_ identities through their choices
of films, personal rating systems, and what they write. An online movie review can then be
considered “a blank canvas” for identity performance.


**Marketplace Cultures**


Another research stream of CCT is Marketplace Cultures, which studies how the rise of
consumer culture constitutes how consumers act and interpret, and how each individual
consumer contributes to the larger marketplace culture (Arnould & Thompson, 2005).
Consumers are considered culture producers, which is in contrast with the traditional
anthropological view of people as culture bearers (Thompson, 2024). Marketplace Cultures
researchers further examine how the pursuit of common consumption interests shapes a sense of
social unity and how consumers create consumption worlds, which are “distinctive, fragmentary,
self-selected, and sometimes transient” (Arnould & Thompson, 2005, p. 873).


An online review platform (such as Letterboxd) can be conceived of as a _consumption world_
(Holt, 1995), a social space organized around consumers' shared interest in movies and critique.
More recent CCT scholarship has extended this concept by examining how consumers in
collaborative networks actively co-create the rules of exchange, often producing _hybrid_
_economies_ that blend market and non-market logics (Scaraboto, 2015). In the context of
Letterboxd, reviewers participate in a similar hybrid economy, as they produce content (reviews)
for free, while the platform commercializes their aggregated activity. In the marketplace culture
of Letterboxd, consumers participate in the ritual of logging and reviewing, which cultivates the
consumer culture of "shared beliefs, meanings, mythologies, rituals, social practices, and status
systems" (Arnould & Thompson, 2005, p. 874).


**Consumer Ideology**


The last relevant research program examines Consumer Ideology, which is conceived of as the
systematic set of beliefs that (re)produce consumers' thoughts and actions in accordance with
consumerism (Arnould & Thompson, 2005). Consumer Ideology focuses on how consumers
make sense and respond to the normative messages produced by commercial media. Ritson &
Elliott’s (1999) research on the perception of advertisements showed that when consumers
critique advertisements in a social context, they are collectively (re-)interpreting the message of a
campaign. In the study of _consumption ideology,_ consumers are positioned as interpretive agents,


7


who engage in meaning-creation activities (Schmitt et al., 2022). The existence of a consumption
ideology is in a way inescapable, as individual interpretive strategies permeate every phase of the
consumer journey, as well as social reality itself (Kozinets, 2002; Schmitt et al., 2022; Žižek,
1997).


The consumption ideology behind movie experiences is then quite relevant to understanding how
online movie reviewers engage with both films and the social reality they find themselves in
(Žižek, 1997). To simplify consumer ideology, Schmitt et al., (2022, p. 62) propose a 2×2 matrix
of consumption orientations. Reconnection is a conformist mental stance embracing consumerist
ideology; alienation is a resistive, anti-ideology mental stance; engagement is conformist
communicative action; and activism is resistive communicative action opposing mainstream
consumerist ideology. Hence, we can examine whether similar consumption orientations apply to
online movie reviews, wherein Letterboxd users are collectively interpreting, critiquing, and
reworking the meanings of films.

### **Studying Movie Reviewing From CCT Perspectives**


The synthesization of CCT research streams allows this study to conceptualize movie reviewing
on Letterboxd as a multifaceted cultural phenomenon. From the perspective of Consumer
Identity Projects, reviewing is a practice through which users construct and perform “cinephile”
identities. From the perspective of Marketplace Cultures, Letterboxd is a "consumption world,"
organized around shared interests, rituals and status systems, wherein users participate in
collective meaning-making through their engagement with the platform and each other. From the
perspective of Consumer Ideologies, reviewers collectively employ interpretive strategies to
create a consensus of opinion.


These CCT perspectives provide this thesis a framework that positions online movie reviewing
as a practice, which is inherently influenced by consumer culture. For this study, CCT is the
fundamental theoretical basis for investigating how online movie reviews assist consumers in
constructing identities, forging a sense of community, negotiating social structures, and engaging
with ideological meanings. This macro-level theoretical framework of CCT complements the
micro-level psychological processes described in the following chapter about the APE model.
Ultimately, CCT perspectives enable us to conduct a multi-level (macro/micro) analysis on how
the process of reviewing transforms movie consumption experience.

## Mapping Movie Evaluations With The APE Model


While CCT provides this thesis a macro-level understanding of reviewing as part of consumer
culture, CCT does not elaborate on the psychological processes underlying individual consumer
evaluations. To address this, we introduce Gawronski & Bodenhausen’s (2006)
Associative-Propositional Evaluation (APE) model, which enables us to conceptualize how


8


movie evaluative judgments are formed through the interaction of two distinct mental processes
(associative/propositional). By focusing in on both a macro-cultural and micro-psychological
level, we try to avoid what Thompson (2024, p. 562) refers to as _cherry picking_ concepts from
CCT “as mere grist for the theory testing mill.” The APE model is used for theorizing the
unobservable processes that generate observable reviews, which is crucial since it is only
possible to “observe the outcome but not the process" (Schneider et al. 2021, p. 585).

### **Associative vs. Propositional Processes**


At the core of the APE model is the mental distinction between _associative processes_ and
_propositional processes_ (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2006). These processes form two distinct
types of evaluations, which Gawronski & Bodenhausen (2006) refer to as implicit and explicit
attitudes. In the case of movie evaluations, involuntary affective reactions are a part of the
implicit attitudes, and deliberative articulated judgments are the explicit attitudes (Schneider et
al., 2021). This distinction is necessary for initially differentiating and, later on, analyzing the
evaluations expressed by the Letterboxd reviewers.


**Associative Processes as The Basis of Implicit Evaluation**


Associative evaluations are characterized by Gawronski & Bodenhausen (2006, p. 693) as
"automatic affective reactions resulting from the particular associations that are activated
automatically when one encounters a relevant stimulus." The words “activated automatically”
are important here, since this means that associative evaluations do not require cognitive
awareness – there is no perceived intention by the consumer to evaluate an object (Cunningham
et al., 2004). Therefore, implicit film evaluations occur when certain associations activate
unconsciously (Schneider et al., 2021). For example, prior knowledge of a director's cinematic
style might evoke certain associations/expectations, whilst such activations occur implicitly.

without conscious effort or deliberate control.


Importantly, associative processes are independent from an individual’s cognitive perception of
what is _actually_ true. Associative evaluations can happen unconsciously, and are not dependent
on whether an individual considers them accurate or not (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2006).
This understanding is key for distinguishing associative processing from propositional reasoning
in the context of movie reviewing. Continuing with the example of a reviewer being familiar
with a director's work, previous disappointing experiences can automatically elicit negative
affective reactions. Similar associations, regardless if positive or negative in nature, activate
automatically, regardless of their perceived validity and/or a conscious recognition of an inherent
bias (Schneider et al., 2021).


The activation of thought patterns are also central to associative processes (Smith, 1996).
Essentially, the creation of an associative evaluation is based on the fit between a set of external


9


input stimuli and the preexisting associations in memory (Schneider et al., 2021). It is then also
the _in vitro_ context wherein the experience happens which determines the recruited associative
patterns (Smith, 1996). The process of evaluating movies might bring forth different associations
when viewed in different contexts (e.g., at a cinema vs. at home), or, more pertinently, when
interacting with an online review platform. The online consumer review platform is then itself a
part of the input stimuli that activates pattern recognition, as well as the remembered

associations.


For the specific platform context of this thesis (Letterboxd), associative processes take form in
the emotional, intuitive elements hidden within the act of reviewing. Reviews which express
visceral reactions (e.g., this LoTR review on Letterboxd: “frodo 🥺 wouldn’t 🥺 have 🥺 got
🥺 far 🥺 without 🥺 sam 🥺 ” _)_ may show emotions which have not been subjected to
deliberative reflection. These emotionally _raw_ evaluations can be conceived of as an expression
of automatic affective reactions, stemming from associative processes. When gathering empirical
materials about the practice of online movie reviewing, implicit evaluations need to be mapped
alongside the explicit evaluations described in the following chapter.


**Propositional Processes as The Basis of Explicit Evaluation**


Propositional processes differ from associative processes, since propositional processes are
concerned with the validation of evaluations and beliefs. The explicit, observable evaluations
resulting from propositional processes, are more akin to “evaluative judgments that are based on
syllogistic inferences derived from any kind of propositional information that is considered
relevant for a given judgment" (Gawronski and Bodenhausen, 2006, p. 694). This means that a
process of deductive reasoning is necessary to reach explicit evaluations, wherein two premises
are used to arrive at a necessary conclusion. Simply put, a (re)viewer must construct a subjective
line of reasoning for expressing their explicit evaluation (e.g., answering the question “Why do I
think this movie is good/bad?”).


In the APE model, propositional reasoning functions are based on associative inputs that have
been modified to fit into a propositional format (Schneider et al., 2021). Following this logic, a
negative affective reaction is transformed into the proposition: "I dislike X," and becomes
subject to deductive logical arguments that assess its validity. For instance, the affective response
of “I love this movie” is either justified with a reason (e.g., “because the acting is great”) or
overruled (e.g., “I actually only love the actor, not the movie”).


The distinctive feature of propositional processes is their dependency on truth values. Whereas
associative activations occur independently of perceived truth or falsity, propositional reasoning
is concerned with validation (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2006). Propositional processes
culminate with the answer to the question: “Is this evaluation valid?” Often by default,
propositional reasoning affirms the validity of their automatic affective reactions (Gilbert, 1991).


10


In such cases, an affective reaction ("I dislike X") is the entire basis of the evaluative judgment.
In other cases, when an automatic affective reaction is rejected as invalid, evaluative judgments
are independent; unaffected by feelings of affection.


The perceived validity of an evaluation also depends on consistency, or rather on whether the
other relevant propositions are in alignment (Schneider et al., 2021). In the case of movie
evaluations, a (re)viewer may have an automatic negative affective reaction to a film featuring a
perceivably “pretentious” or a “bad” director. However, this reaction may be inconsistent with
other propositions they hold. For instance, they might have been affected by the external
validation a film received, such as the film was “good enough” to win an Oscar or has high
praise within the Letterboxd community. Inconsistencies among propositions can result in the
rejection of the affective reaction as an invalid basis for judgment, resulting in a different explicit
evaluation than their implicit reaction would predict (Schneider et al., 2021). Alternatively, a
(re)viewer can also find an additional proposition that resolves the inconsistency (e.g., "This
director has actually improved significantly over time"). The additional proposition confirms the
negative affective reaction as valid, which leads to alignment between the implicit and explicit

evaluation.


In the context of CCT, explicit movie evaluations are affected by the “consumption world,” and
are displayed in review texts through the result of propositional reasoning processes. More
specifically, propositional processes are the analytical, reflective, and argumentative elements
embedded within the review process. Propositional reasoning provides agency to movie
(re)viewers to either validate or reject their initial affective reactions against other relevant
propositions, according to their own volition.

**The Transformational Outcomes of Movie Reviewing**


When movie consumers engage in the review process, it is possible to see their
(re-)interpretations of evaluations and how reviewing can transform a movie consumption
experience. Based on the reviewed literature, we posit three following transformational outcomes
of movie reviewing that alter the consumption experience itself:


**Deepening Consumption Experiences** : Reviewing requires reflection and articulation
(described by Schneider et al., 2021 as propositional reasoning), which prolongs engagement
with the experience. The development of propositional reasoning through reviewing is then how
movie reviewers transform their consumption experience.


**(Re-)Constructing Consumption Memories & Meanings:** While not directly addressed in
either the APE model or CCT, autobiographical memory theory is also an essential part of
consumer evaluations. Tulving (2002) has discussed how past consumption experiences are
stored as episodic traces that are reconstructed each time they are retrieved. This line of research
was further developed by Moliner-Tena et al., (2023), who explain that episodic/autobiographical
memory is “a subsystem of Long Term Memory (LTM) where associations of an
autobiographical event relating to time, space, emotions or other contextual circumstances are
stored” (pg. 2). From this line of psychological research, a written online review is one part of
the constant construction of LTM, which anchors explicit evaluations in-time.


Furthermore, psychological researchers use the term _memory phenomenology_ (Moliner-Tena et
al., 2023) to describe the subjective experience of reliving an experience. As was also partly
covered by Schneider et al. (2021), two dimensions of memory phenomenology are especially
relevant to movie evaluation/reviewing. _Vividness_ of a memory reflects the ability to recall
details of specific consumption experiences. (Cooper et al., 2019). _Sharing_ considers whether
and how the experience was shared with other people (Sutin & Robins, 2007). Reviewing can
elicit _vividness,_ as reviewers are prompted to recall their experience, but when combined with an

‑
online platform they are also _sharing_ it. Moliner Tena et al. (2023, p. 3) say that _sharing_,
“enables greater social integration and […] activates the sharing dimension” of autobiographical
memory. Also we must not forget to mention the forgetting theory, which states that memories
_decay_ over time or become harder to retrieve because other memories induce _interference_
(Jonides et al., 2008).


Exceptionally vivid recollections of surprising or significant experiences are known as _flashbulb_
_events_, which can be defined as “an extreme case of the memory of an event, which take the

‑
shape of extremely vivid, detailed and emotional memories” (Moliner Tena et al., 2023, p. 2).
Developing a system of propositional reasoning can make reviews function as a _flashbulb event_
like memory by adding layers of personal significance to prior experiences (Schneider et al.,
2021).


14


Ultimately this transformational outcome posits that a review is a shared, vivid reconstruction of

‑
a consumption experience. This aligns with Moliner Tena et al.’s (2023) broader claim about
how a memory theory perspective enables a connection between human memory theory and

‑
service dominant logic. The review process enables consumers to engage in active

‑
meaning making, allowing for them to encapsulate and remember personal consumption

memories.


**Performance of Identity in a Consumer Community** : Online reviews are shared in certain
consumer communities (e.g., Letterboxd), and are therefore inherently social performances. The
awareness of the “audience” shapes reviews and turns the expression of a consumption
experience into an opportunity for expressing Consumer Ideologies and a sense of one’s identity
in relation to other consumption world members.


**The Cycle of Consumption**


The presented model (Figure 1) implies a cyclical dynamic. Online reviews lead back into the
CCT macro-level: becoming a part of the consumption world discourse, influencing consumption
norms, and shaping the content that other community members encounter. The micro level is also
affected, since past review experiences influence both associative and propositional processes.
The cyclical view is in alignment with the thesis’s central premise that an art consumption

‑
experience is not only the viewing experience, but instead a co creative process, happening
ongoingly through interactions with art and within the consumption world.

## How to Review Reviews: Research Design


To investigate how the practice of online reviewing transforms movie consumption experiences,
this study first uses netnography: a qualitative research method adapted from ethnography to
study online communities (Kozinets, 2002). Netnography is particularly suited to our research
aim because it provides a window into naturally occurring, unsolicited consumer behaviors that
are not fabricated by the researcher. Netnography allows for the unobtrusive observation of
consumers engaging in communal meaning-making within their native digital environment,
offering access to the symbolism, linguistic conventions, and consumption patterns of online
consumer groups (Kozinets, 2002). For this thesis, netnography enabled us to collect and
interpret Letterboxd user reviews as cultural performances embedded within a consumption
world (Kozinets, 2002).


The research is structured as a two-phase, iterative study, combining netnography with
semi-structured interviews. The dual-method approach allows for more context about users
themselves and their reviews, as we are able to first familiarize ourselves with user reviews and


15


then follow up with interviews for more in-depth, self-reflective insights. This enables us to
better understand the motivations and introspections underlying users’ public reviews.

### **Recruitment of Interview Participants**


Recruiting participants directly through Letterboxd was challenging due to the platform’s
structural limitations. Unlike any other social media, Letterboxd does not offer the option of
direct messaging or any chat feature. If users did not include external contact information such as
email addresses or social media handles, it was impossible to reach out to them. This made it
difficult to target individual users directly on the platform without leaving public comments on
their reviews. This approach felt intrusive and unlikely to lead to any meaningful responses.


We chose TikTok as an alternative channel to recruit potential interviewees. mostly because of its
active cinephile communities. We used hashtags while conducting research which included:
#letterboxd, #moviereview, #filmtok, #cinemalover over a four-week period in April 2026.
Public videos with content regarding movies and Letterboxd reviewing on that platform were
used to screen for relevance. Furthermore, potential candidates were contacted through TikTok
direct messages with a brief invitation to the study. In total around 200 people were contacted

which resulted in 13 interviews. No incentives were offered.

### **Participant Diversity**


The final sample of 13 participants was intentionally diverse in terms of nationality, reviewing
background and also engagement on the platform. Participants were from nine different
countries: the United Kingdom, the United States, Spain, France, Germany, Sweden, Poland,
Venezuela and Estonia. The variety of nationalities have provided a broad range of different
cultural perspectives on online film reviewing and movie consumption.


On top of that the sample included participants with different approaches to the reviewing
experience and online presence. Some of the interviewees belonged to the group of large creators
with a significant amount of followers on platforms like TikTok and Letterboxd. They regularly
received press invitations to attend premiere screenings and collaborated with film distribution.
Furthermore, we collaborated with smaller creators with lesser following on Letterboxd who
reviewed for small-scale community engagement or their own satisfaction. Others could be
named as lone archivists whose relation with Letterboxd is more private, as they used Letterboxd
primarily as a logging tool, with little to no interest in social engagement on the platform. The
study also included professional or semi-professional reviewers, often film students or video
editors. Another group consisted of casual viewers who review sporadically when they feel like
it and mostly for films that leave a strong emotional impact.


16


Some of the participants used Letterboxd as their main “movie” platform, while others used it in
the meantime while relying more on TikTok or Youtube. Interviewees attitudes towards
reviewing practice were diverse. Some of them were analytical and serious, whereas other users
logged one-line or joke reviews. This heterogeneity was intentional, reflecting CCT’s
“distributed view of cultural meaning” (Arnould & Thompson, 2005).

### **Interview Procedure**


All of the interviews were conducted online between March and April 2026. To ensure
interviewees comfort, we used a range of platforms to meet their preferred one, such as: Zoom,
Microsoft Teams, Discord, Google Meet and FaceTime audio. In the end the specific platform
was chosen by the participant. Each interview lasted from 30 to 80 minutes.


All interviews were audio-recorded with the usage of OBS (Open Broadcaster Software) and
later transcribed using ElevenLabs, a professional transcription tool, and manually corrected with

any errors.


The semi-structured interviews were guided by the open-ended questions, which allowed to
better understand and explore reviewers perspectives. The complete interview guide is provided

### **Trustworthiness**





To ensure the trustworthiness of our thesis, we followed the framework proposed by Lincoln and
Guba (operationalised by Shenton, 2004). We address four criteria: credibility, transferability,
dependability, and confirmability.


**Credibility**


While we employed several strategies to ensure our findings credibility to represent a true picture
of the phenomenon under scrutiny (Shenton, 2004), we must acknowledge a significant sampling
bias. Reviewers and their reviews were intentionally selected for characteristics that align with
the very phenomena we set out to study. This means our findings cannot be generalized to all
Letterboxd reviewers, as we have heavily prioritized theory-building in our research design and
not representativeness.


We combined netnographic analysis of 260 Letterboxd reviews with semi-structured interviews
of 13 active users to compensate for the limitations of one singular research approach (Shenton,
2004). We tried to achieve data source triangulation by recruiting a diverse range of users (9
different countries, different reviewing habits). As we experienced difficulties in initially
recruiting participants, our cohort of interview participants were skewed towards engaged users,
who were willing to express themselves in a research context.


We also applied Shenton’s (2004, p. 66) “tactics to help ensure honesty in informants.”
Participants were informed they could (1) withdraw from our thesis project at any point; (2)
express themselves freely; (3) provide no "wrong answers" (Shenton, 2004).


19


“Peer scrutiny” (Shenton, 2004, p. 67) of the thesis project was achieved through regular
discussions with our thesis supervisor Peter Zackariasson. We received feedback on our research
progress and use of theory, which we used to strengthen our arguments and final text.


Peer scrutiny was accompanied by a reflective commentary throughout our research process. To
record "progressive subjectivity" (Shenton, 2004, p. 68) we kept a track of emerging patterns in
the data and our evolving interpretations.


**Transferability**


Since this thesis covers specifically movie reviewing on Letterboxd, the transferability of our
findings to other situations have to be made clear (Shenton, 2004). Our qualitative findings are
specific to the thesis’ research context: studied specifically the Letterboxd platform; used
theories from CCT and APE-model, employed netnography and semi-structured interviews for
research material collection; interviewed 13 active users of 9 different nationalities; analyzed 260
reviews; conducted research in April 2026. This “thick description” of our research context
allows readers to assess the transferability of this thesis’ findings to other situations (Shenton,
2004, p. 69).


**Dependability**


To address the dependability of our thesis, we have reported our research processes in detail in
our methodological chapter. This was done so future research can be conducted in a similar

fashion in efforts to see if similar results would be obtained . This enables a future researcher to

repeat the work, if not necessarily to obtain the same results (Shenton, 2004).


**Confirmability**


The confirmability of our research depends on the extent to which we were able to convey true
experiences of our interview participants (Shenton, 2004). While we acknowledge our inherent
investigator bias, we took several measures to promote confirmability. Triangulation was used to
reduce the effect of investigator bias by incorporating multiple methods and data sources
(Shenton, 2004). Our methodological decisions developed through scrutiny and open discussion
with the thesis supervisor and we recorded our analysis progress throughout. As suggested by
Shenton (2004), an audit trail documenting analytical decisions, coding iterations, and
methodological choices has been maintained and is available upon request. Nevertheless, we
acknowledge several limitations of our research, which we cover in detail in the Discussion
chapter.

### **Theme 1: Reviewing Extends and Deepens the Consumption Experience**


The first transformational outcome (described in Figure 1) posits that the act of reviewing
prolongs engagement with the reviewed artwork and transforms a one-time experience into a
process of meaning making. Our interview participants described how reviewing adds something
to the consumption experience, which passive consumption lacks. The way in which the
consumption experience was affected by reviewing varied across participants, which we
categorized as affective prolongation or cognitive prolongation.


**Associative Processes’ Relation to Affective Prolongation**


When participants were asked “what does reviewing mean to you,” many described what we
categorized as affective prolongation. Reviewing allowed immediate (associative) reactions to be
processed and put into a personal context.


_“When I review, I feel like I’m releasing those emotions… It feels like a closing chapter to having_
_experienced some great art.”_ - user mvrtsysy


A review of _Die My Love_ (2025) exemplifies what we mean by affective prolongation.
Immediately after watching, the reviewer “didn't feel anything,” which we interpreted as an
associative evaluation of numbness. Over several days, a "long deep feeling" developed, growing
stronger until user mvrtsysy felt almost “compelled to write.” The act of writing forced reflection
and produced what the user describes as “catharsis” and “closure.” Without the review, user
mvrtsysy suggested that “emotional baggage” would remain unresolved.


_“In the beginning, after just watching the movie, I didn't feel anything. But this feeling_
_came up after like two days and only grew stronger and at one point it was so strong I felt_
_like, ‘Okay, I have to write a review to get rid of it [the feeling] and to deal with it.’ ”_ 
user mvrtsysy


This example does not mean that articulated reviews always lead to further understanding, which

some reviews showcased:


_“This movie felt so genuine, I don’t know how they did it…”_ - user mvrtsysy review of _Almost_
_Famous_ (2000)


22


Other participants also described the reviewing process through cathartic language, helping them
deal with feelings that otherwise could remain internally unresolved. For some, articulating
symbolism and personal connections to the artwork allowed them to externalize lingering
feelings. User dila_ern explained that they often write reviews right after crying during a film,
since the associative reaction compels them to engage in propositional reasoning.


_“And when I, when there is a sign of me crying, I immediately put my thoughts because, like, why_
_did I cry? Why was I so emotional?“_ - user dila_ern


In another example, dila_ern described being motivated to review a film through feelings of
“female rage”:


_“It was, like, 3:00 AM in the morning and it really motivated me to put my thoughts into the_
_review, and it was driven by my female rage, I would say, and how awful humanity can be, how_
_cruel it can be.”_ - user dila_ern


Since these emotions were so intense and difficult, reviewing became a medium to deal with
them. Importantly, some reviews are to resolve the emotional reaction it produced. The
reviewing/writing process became a way for participants to reflect on why certain emotions
carried such intensity, where they originated from, and, at a core, what they revealed about
themselves. While we cannot directly observe the automatic processing that is happening during
the review process, participants' descriptions of visceral reactions provide indirect evidence of its

role.


A reviewing practice can then be a vehicle for how consumers resolve and process emotions felt
through movie consumption experiences. Associative movie evaluations (immediate, visceral
reactions) can be overwhelming, and the emotional weight from the movie consumption
experience can last for a longer period of time. Engaging with the review process is then how the
automatic processing of associative evaluations is being subconsciously developed, and how

future associative evaluations are manifested.


**Propositional Reasoning’s Relation to Cognitive Prolongation**


The deepening effect of reviewing can also be primarily cognitive, differing from the affective
prolongation. Unlike reviewers who write to vent or to analyze, a reviewing practice can in some
cases resemble more of a film celebration, where it builds up on emotional energy.


_“When I come out of a film and I've just thoroughly enjoyed it and I'm writing a review about_
_how much I loved it, it definitely does intensify. It's just like a love letter to the film. It does make_
_me feel a more intense feeling toward the film.”_ – user jophesface


What makes this finding interesting is that, for some, reviewing changes opinions on the film for

the better.


23


_“Upon rewatch, when I was writing my second review, I was realizing how great it is and how_
_much I just love all the decisions in the film. It definitely did make me realize I enjoyed it a lot_
_more than my initial thoughts.”_ - user jophesface


Here reviewing constructs an enhanced reimagination of a movie, as writing about enjoyment
can generate more enjoyment, leading to more enthusiasm about reviewing practice and movie
consumption.


For others, a reviewing practice was a method for achieving a sense of critical understanding of
the movie consumption experience.


_“Usually, it turns out that I like movies less ... because I tend to overanalyze.”_ - anonymous user


A retelling of a review of the movie _Sinister_ (2012), illustrates this aspect. While initially
captivated by the premise (an associative evaluation), a written analysis concluded that “the
demon plot was lame” and the overall execution flawed. Here, the deepened engagement
produced critical disappointment instead of “catharsis” or “positive valence.” This is relevant to
the covered CCT theory, because the valence of prolongation (how consumption experience is
perceived as more or less positive) depended on the reviewer's lived experience. Relating this to
the APE model, we see how the developing propositional reasoning system either overrides
positive associative reactions, or affirms and deepens them.


A continuous reviewing practice can also develop a sense of personal cinematic quality over

time.


_“Before starting reviewing, I had a bunch of movies that were four plus stars ... But after starting_
_to review, I have realized quite some shortcomings.”_ - user hermitmaxxr2000


Here engaging in the reviewing process directly shapes future viewing experience, wherein:


_“The reviewing process just doesn't start when the movie ends ... by the time the movie ends, I_
_already kind of know what I want to review, what I liked and what I disliked.”_ - user

hermitmaxxr2000


For some reviewers, the anticipated act of writing creates a continuous feedback loop between
watching and evaluating. The reviewer becomes a more attentive viewer, since they have
previously developed their propositional reasoning capabilities through reviewing.


User heythisisjames described a similar occurrence, but from a social angle. When they started
reviewing films, they paid attention to what others would think of their reviews. Over time, they
learned to ignore any pressure that might have been caused by people and started to rely more on
their own reasoning.


24


_“I certainly did think about, 'What will people think about this?' when I started. And then I think_
_I didn't learn how to let that go until maybe a year into doing TikTok.”_ - user heythisisjames


This implies that the development of propositional reasoning from a reviewing practice is not
only personal but also social. Reviewers learn to validate their own judgments against the
imagined audience’s potential criticism.


‑
Self awareness can also align with the development of propositional reasoning:


_“I'll always come up with a number after a film for the star rating. But then when I'm actually_
_laying out my opinions, I'm sort of like, 'Oh, yeah, I actually thought this…' Like, when the stuff_
_I'm saying, it doesn't actually sound that good. I do think the ones where I write a longer review_
_are more informed.”_ - user jophesface


Initial star ratings can often be misleading, and engaging in the review practice produces a “more
informed” judgment. This is directly associated with how propositional reasoning can override

initial associative evaluations.


**How Online Reviewing Prolongs Engagement with Movie Consumption Experiences**


All participants exhibited some form of prolonged engagement from an active practice of
reviewing. This does not mean that there is a uniform deepening of experience, as reviewers
have different meanings for their personal movie reviewing practice. This finding is then in line
with CCT’s central premise of “the distributed view of cultural meaning” (Arnould & Thompson,
2005).


Central to this theme is how future reviews were affected by the gained reviewing experience. In
some cases, the maturing of the reviewer’s reviewing process evoked better understanding of
associative evaluations and the development of propositional reasoning capabilities. In regards to

‑
the multi level model proposed in Figure 1, the movie consumer/reviewer identity is in a
constant state of evolution. Through each articulation of movie consumption experiences,
consumers create their identity project, develop their evaluative disposition, and their co-creative
relations to the consumption world they are situated within.

### **Theme 2: Reviewing Reconstructs Memory and Meaning**


The next analysis theme draws from memory reconstruction theory (Jonas et al., 2023;
Moliner-Tena et al., 2023). Our findings showed how reviewers instill self-identity within some
reviews, and how movie reviewing can overwrite or solidify the memory of a consumption
experience. This was expressed by the interview participants in 3 ways: rewriting significance,
temporal anchoring, and humorous encoding.


25


**The Significance of Rewriting**


One user’s experience with the film _Stoker_ (2013) was an especially vivid example of reviewing
impacting the personal significance of a film. User mvrtsysy watched the movie with no
expectations, enjoyed it, but did not consider it to be “relevant.” A realization of a deep
connection with the film occurred after engaging with the review process:


_“Even after watching it… I was like, ‘this is still not the most relevant movie.’ But after I wrote a_
_review, I realized I actually connect more to it than I thought.”_ - user mvrtsysy


We found other examples of similar experiences, wherein a reviewing practice is the core part of
movie consumption experiences. User heythisisjames described how reviewing could alter the
intensity and direction of their feelings toward a movie: “in reflection, it can sometimes be quite
different or even greater or lesser." Rather than only documenting reactions formed during
viewing, the writing process influences the interpretation and evaluation of the film, sometimes
helping them realize how they actually felt about the experience. However, the direction and
magnitude of this intensification differed between users and situations. In user heythisisjames’
case, it moved toward a more negative direction. Reflecting on the film, they realized that they
“actually don't have really anything positive to say,” eventually concluding that “that's when the
clarity kind of kicks in” that they “did not like this film or this experience.” This finding further
emphasizes the importance of the APE model when it comes to the consumer-centric view on the
impacts of online movie reviewing. Reviewing forces propositional reasoning, which in turn
validates/rejects associative evaluations, and develops the self-identity of the movie consumer.


A contrary finding to what we assumed based on consumer memory literature is how the actual
movie consumption experience can be overshadowed by the review itself.


_“I wrote a review, and I think that review shaped me more than the movie I think. I can't recall_
_the review, but it gave me this feeling.”_ - user dila_ern


This example shows how highly dedicated reviewers can focus on the creative outlet the
reviewing process provides, which then becomes more significant than watching the movie.
Memory decay after reviewing still occurs, possibly because other reviewing related memories
induce interference (Moliner-Tena et al., 2023). The memory of the review is then more
substantial than the consumption experience itself and the meaning of the consumption
experience comes more from the review than the actual movie.


We did see support for the idea that reviews rewrite the film’s significance retroactively, which is
consistent with the APE model’s propositional override: associative inputs can be rejected as
invalid, and a new explicit judgment constructed through reasoning (Schneider et al., 2021). The
review becomes the official evaluation of the experience, which can be recalled in the future. For
example, another review of the movie _Die My Love_ (2025) transformed “an initial sense of


26


irrelevance” into a "devastatingly accurate" portrayal of mental illness. As was the case with
other users, the review process constructs a response that is more articulate, more meaningful,
and more memorable than the initial, _raw_ experience itself.


**Temporal Anchoring**


Reviewing also places films to the reviewer’s personal timeline, which is inherently related to the
concept of episodic traces from autobiographical memory theory (Tulving, 2002).


_“I check my timeline… I see in what months I saw what kind of movies. And then I sometimes_
_relive those months and that time in my head again.”_ - user hermitmaxxr2000


Through coding, we identified a pattern of reviews as temporal anchors, akin to fixed episodic
traces, which allow for the navigation of past consumption experiences. Reviewed movies are
remembered for their content, as well as, for the life context in which they are experienced. In
contrast, unreviewed movies are not shared nor vividly expressed through a review, and are
susceptible to memory decay:


_“There's so many movies, like maybe even thousands of movies that I've watched, and I just don't_
_remember them because I never thought about them more.”_ - anonymous user


Furthermore, we observed that writing a review forced a deliberate, effortful retrieval of a
memory, strengthening episodic traces as temporal anchors. This can make it less vulnerable to
memory decay and interference, as was expressed clearly by one of our interviewees:


_“If I leave a review, I'm going to mentally enforce and solidify that criticism... even when years_
_go by, I know where it landed, like, was it four stars, three stars, then I automatically know, like,_
_oh, okay, it maybe was an okay movie.”_ - user hermitmaxxr2000


Even just a star rating itself can become a temporal anchor that solidifies the evaluative
assessment of experience. This suggests that the quantified aspect of reviewing (star ratings)
does not only serve an aggregative function but also as a mnemonic device for the reviewer.


_“When I come back to a review from years ago, I can see my thoughts and be like, 'Oh, I still_
_agree with myself,' or 'Oh, I changed my mind.' It's nice in retrospect to see the list of movies_
_you've reviewed and being like, 'Oh, I remember this.'”_ - user heythisisjames


A recorded review makes a movie consumption revisitable, as past identities can be felt again
when re-reading past reviews. This has the ability to prevent memory decay and aligns with the
APE model’s propositional reasoning, since writing forces the reviewer to validate/reject
associative evaluations. Previous reviews can be perceived as a mirror, where a reviewer can
check what they thought about a movie but also who they were as a person at a certain time of


27


his life. This occurrence links to Consumer Identity Projects, where the review becomes a
recorded file of the one's evolving self-identity.


_“If I write a review, it's in my daily life like that. If I experience something really important or_
_really life-changing, I have a lot of thoughts and feelings, and I typically write them down, and_
_then they are, like, anchored on a page and I can look back with the feelings just running over_
_me.” -_ user dila_ern


**Humorous Encoding**


Other reviewers demonstrate that even minimal or comedic reviews act as a memory

**How Memory Reconstruction Constructs Consumer Experiences**


Analysis theme 2 supports the memory reconstruction transformational outcome of the proposed
model (Figure 1), in that memory reconstruction is central to how consumer identities are
formed, conserved and shared. Our analysis showed how the continuation of a reviewing practice


28


can enhance a consumer's ability to consolidate opinions, anchor them in a personal timeline, and
express them in an acceptable form.


The movie consumption experience altering effects induced by reviewing are backed up in our
analysis by concepts from autobiographical memory theory. Not only did our cohort of reviewers
better remember their art consumption experience upon leaving a review, they are also
developing a timeline of themselves as movie consumers. The changing nature of
consumer/reviewer identities are encapsulated on their Letterboxd profiles, which gives credence
to the idea that Consumer Ideologies (CI) (Arnould & Thompson, 2005) are being continuously
developed through the reviewing process and by consumers maturing as reviewers.

### **Theme 3: Reviewing as Performance of Identity and Community**


In our analysis, we observed a multitude of ways in which online reviewing expresses social
performances of identity and community culture. Across interview participants distinct identities
were performed in different ways, ranging from community oriented authenticity to deliberate
privacy to ironic detachment. Our findings cover different methods of positioning oneself within
the Letterboxd consumption world, and how the culture of the platform shapes how movie
consumers want to be perceived amongst peers.


**Performing Authenticity**


Many of the interview participants claimed that for them there is no inherent intention to “seek
likes” and Letterboxd functions more as a “personal journal.” Nevertheless, the analysed reviews
were filled with direct addresses to readers, rhetorical questions, and explicit engagement with
community discourse. This highlights the inescapability of performance within public reviewing
practices, wherein supposedly private reflections become shaped by the awareness of reaching

out to an online audience.


One concern that emerged from exposure to other reviewers’ opinions was a fear of interpretive
inauthenticity. User mvrtsysy described uncertainty over whether their thoughts were genuinely
their own after being exposed to numerous existing opinions and interpretations. If that feeling
persisted, they would sometimes feel reluctant to even write the review altogether:


_“Sometimes I hold myself back because so many people have already written it, and I feel like_
_my thoughts are not authentic.”_ - user mvrtsysy


This suggests that the feedback loop created through reviewing can also become directed toward
the self, not only toward the artwork or the audience. Letterboxd users consume not only films,
but also pre-existing interpretations. The abundance of available personal reviews can destabilize
their confidence in the originality of their reactions, introducing a form of meta-cognition that
shifts from “What do I think?” to “Did I actually think it myself?”. Therefore, reviewing can


29


shape not only how consumers experience film, but also how they experience themselves as
interpreters of it.


Other participants also expressed a fear of redundancy, which can be interpreted as a direct
consequence of audience awareness. The imagined community is the benchmark against which
users measure their performance authenticity.


_“What was I going to say about Forrest Gump that hasn't been said?”_ - user joyplague


Seemingly, it is the expansiveness of the Letterboxd consumption world that creates the pressure
toward originality. To be worthwhile or necessary, a review must add something new to the
conversation. This pressure is distinctly a social constraint, which emerges from the communal
nature of the platform and constant exposure to existing interpretations. The social pressure
attached to acting as an early interpreter appeared to further deepen consumption experience.
Reviewing requires both an individual reflective process, as well as a social act of cultural
mediation. Therefore, each reviewer is affected by the larger marketplace culture, and has an
active role in shaping how films are interpreted within it.


**Performing Taste and Cinephilic Legitimacy**


Interview participants also reflected on authenticity through their relationship to cinephilic norms
and reviewer legitimacy. Some users expressed the pressure of engaging with films, especially
highly critically acclaimed ones, in the “correct” way. It became particularly visible the moment
participants gained larger audiences and became more conscious about the weight their voice
would carry as cultural value.


User elementarii described how she managed to break this cycle and gradually distanced herself
from viewing films through a critic-oriented lens:


_“I was really watching it like a critic, and it became very unenjoyable.” -_ user elementarii


They explained that instead of pressuring herself to constantly consume “really good films” or
“very highbrow cinema,” they began selecting films based on mood and personal enjoyment.
Authenticity was therefore expressed through resisting institutionalized critic norms and
allowing being emotionally subjective in the viewing experience.


At the same time, other participants remained aware of the broader cinephilic value systems.
Especially, when reviewing culturally significant films, they expressed a tension between natural
reactions and the mirrored cinephilic legitimacy. User dila_ern described questioning her own
rating after giving an acclaimed classic the same score as a more widely discredited film:​

_“Why did I give that great classic the same amount of stars as The Housemaid?”_ - user dila_ern


30


The need to revisit that evaluation shows how communal standards can shape perceptions of
what makes a “valid” reaction. This introduces the notion of “cinephilic hive mindset,” where the
reviews become intertwined with what the reviewer believes a valid cinematic critique is.


_“i don't see the hype tbh SOZ :DDD storywise lacking.”_ - user hermitmaxxr2000’s review of
_Ghost in the Shell_ (1995)


Participants negotiated their cinephile identity in different, sometimes opposing ways. User
elementarii associated the label with people who “really know what they’re talking about” or
possess stronger historical and analytical knowledge of cinema, eventually distancing herself
from the identity at all:


_“I don’t think I would apply that term to myself anymore really. I just would say that I love_

_movies.”_ - user elementarii


In contrast, other participants openly embraced the cinephile identity. User heythisisjames
described cinephilia less through expertise or very refined taste, and more as cinema becoming
“a significant part” of one’s life:


_“As soon as you step outside of that norm, you’re kind of entering that field.”_ - user
heythisisjames


These contrasting views demonstrate that authenticity within the Letterboxd consumption world
is not fixed. While some participants pursued authenticity through resisting critic norms, others
expressed it opposingly through highly intentional engagement with cinema.


Ultimately, reviewers collectively shape and reproduce the communal understanding of what
counts as valuable criticism, “good taste,” or legitimate engagement with film. This closely
aligns with Consumer Ideology and Marketplace Culture theory: how reviewers create cinephilic
norms, follow them, negotiate them, and reinforce them in the end through participation in
Letterboxd consumption culture. A performance of authenticity in reviews is then also
juxtaposed with a rejection or a validation of the cinephilic identity.


**Performing Privacy**


Other participants represent the opposite side of online reviewing, specifically a performance of
privacy within a public platform (e.g., “I follow only close friends”).


_“I don’t really care about the community value of Letterboxd at all … it’s purely for me, it’s just_
_my diary.” -_ anonymous user


A performance of privacy is itself a recognizable role within the consumption world. We
detected a pattern of reviewers who were akin to lone archivists, using the platform as a tool,


31


instead of a social space. Nevertheless, even this rejection of community is a form of positioning
since these reviewers rarely address an audience, instead opting for signaling a membership in an
_in-group_ rather than the broader platform.


There are reviewers who occupy a middle ground between the two extremes, valuing some
communal aspects:


_“For me, it's not so much the cinephile communal aspect, or like the Letterboxd communal_
_aspect, like the digital one, more like the, if you meet people in real life who ... are connected to_

_it.” -_ user hermitmaxxr2000


A distinction between digital community and real-life cultural participation is an important
nuance. Although some reviewers do seek validation on the online platform, others use the
platform as a way to express their identity to others offline. Reviewers who felt a connection
between their online and offline identities expressed a feeling of “maturing” as a reviewer and
person, when writing about the artwork.


**Performing Comedy, Relatability and Helpfulness**


Some reviewers were also overtly performative, meaning that their reviews functioned as
addresses to specific audiences, a generic “reader” who will appreciate a carefully crafted take

on a movie.


“ _My fragile identity is very closely tied with comedy no matter the situation, and my reviews_
_reflect that.” -_ anonymous user


These reviewers communicate through memes, ironic undercutting, and pop culture references.
Their humor functions as a mask for genuine passion:


_“I try to be easy going but I am also very passionate. To balance that I tend to be quite ironic.”_ 
anonymous user


On occasion users also exhibited a literal review persona, wherein relatability to the reader of the
review was key. User someone272 considers reviews to be inherently practical, functioning as
recommendations. User joyplague also supports this idea, saying that his reviews are at times a
guide to readers. Helpfulness is hence also a guiding factor for some reviews, but this is more
apparent in retail goods reviews (e.g., Babić Rosario et al., 2020).


**How Reviewing Performs Identities and Constructs Consumption Worlds**


The practice of reviewing allowed our studied cohort of Letterboxd reviewers to express their
identities within a consumption world, thereby influencing Marketplace Cultures. Across our
sample of Letterboxd reviewers a theme of identity performances was apparent, even though


32


unintentional at times. The ways in which these performances unfold were radically different, but
these individual reviewer identities are the building blocks for constructing and maintaining the
consumption world of Letterboxd. As Arnould and Thompson (2005) argue, marketplace
cultures are not static, instead they are continuously produced and reproduced through the
practices of consumers. Each review, whether authentic, private, comedic, or helpful, contributes
to the shared zeitgeist of acceptable ways to be a film reviewer. Following that same logic, over
time, these individual performances become norms and status systems.


Lastly, and perhaps most importantly, the performance of identity does not occur in a vacuum.
Reviewing is shaped by the material affordances of the platform (e.g., the star rating system, the
comment section, the ability to follow other users) and also by the perceived existence of an
audience. Even when reviewers claim to ignore that audience, they are nonetheless responding to
it. This reciprocative relationship between individual performance and collective structure is how
consumption worlds are built, contested, and transformed.

## Discussion


_This thesis set out to answer the question: How does the practice of online reviewing affect_
_movie consumption experiences? Our analysis of Letterboxd reviews and user interviews_
_explained how an active online reviewing practice can induce transformational outcomes, which_
_we discuss in relation to both theoretical and practical implications, as well as limitations and_
_future research._


**Eudaimonic Benefits of Online Movie Reviewing**


In our first analysis theme we integrate our findings into the Figure 1 model and theorize how
reviewing induced affective and cognitive prolongation extends engagement with a movie
consumption experience. Our findings did not indicate that a reviewing practice has a necessarily
positive effect on the consumption experience. However, critical understanding is necessary to
reach conclusive, articulated movie reviews, which does entail the development of propositional
reasoning.


‑
Even comedic or one sentence reviews forced propositional reasoning in order to answer the
question: “What did I think and why?” This supports the APE model’s claim that explicit
evaluation requires validation of associative inputs (Gawronski & Bodenhausen, 2006). Over
time, a system of propositional reasoning is developed, as reviewers become more attentive and

‑
analytically self aware during the viewing itself.


The consumption orientations, as discussed by Schmitt et al. (2022), help us to systematize the
varied reviewing orientations we observed. _Reconnection_ was exhibited by reviewers who


33


embraced Letterboxd's reviewing culture non-reflectively, using it as a personal diary _Alienation_
was expressed as dissatisfaction with the platform's commercial pressures and with the perceived
expectation for originality. _Engagement_ was evident in reviewers who strategically craft reviews
for social validation, and _activism_ in those who deliberately reject community norms. However,
our findings of consumption orientations are not permanent nor rigid, since reviewers can shift

their consumer identities over time or across contexts. This reflects the dialectic nature of
consumption ideology (Žižek, 1997).


Based on our findings it is possible to discuss the eudaimonic benefits of movie reviewing. As
previously covered by Schneider et al. (2021), entertainment experiences can elicit happiness
from meaning and self-realization. When a movie reviewing practice develops consumers’
propositional reasoning, reviewers are able to understand and create authentic self-identities. The
realization and enactment of unique consumption preferences through movie reviewing is what
we think actualizes the perceived eudaimonic benefits.


**Reviews As Temporal Anchors of Past Self-Identities**


Our second analysis theme covered how reviewing elicits the creation of temporal anchors and
encapsulates past self-identities within archived reviews. Memory decay was not necessarily
prevented through reviewing, but previous lines of thinking were made visible to movie
consumers as their archive of reviews. Past movie consumption experiences were not necessarily
more vivid/memorable due to reviewing, but the sharing of movie experiences publicly did
necessitate a reconstruction of episodic traces.


Our findings signify the importance of autobiographical memory theory and Consumer Identity
Projects in movie marketing research. A continuous reviewing practice crystallizes the
self-identity of a movie consumer into a certain point in time. Since reviewers have the ability to
revisit their old reviews, they are able to relive not only the specific movie experience but also a
sense of their past selves. An active reviewing is then directly related to memory reconstruction
and identity work, as it shows the development of propositional reasoning through time.


**The Co-Creation of a Consumption World Through Online Reviewing**


Our third analysis theme relates to how marketplace cultures and consumer ideologies are being
actively co-created. The Letterboxd platform, as the consumption world, is where perceived
norms (e.g., originality, authenticity, humour) are performed. Each new review is a tiny drop in
the ocean of the established Letterboxd reviewing culture and reviewers are consistently
navigating within the expanse of different review ideologies. This was conveyed by interviewees
as a sense of pressure to avoid redundancy or to craft distinct voices that are held to sensed

standards.


34


The nature of our findings brings into question why there is such limited marketing research on
the CCT aspects of online movie reviewing. Researching the consumer experience effects of
online movie reviewing might not necessarily increase box office outcomes, but still offer
insights into the cultural aspects of media consumption. While we acknowledge our qualitative
findings are contingent on the theories we have used in Figure 1, our perceived transformational
outcomes of online movie reviewing still warrant an increased academic interest.

### **Theoretical Contributions**


A major theoretical contribution of our thesis is the integration of CCT and the APE model,

‑ ‑
where the multi level framework we created in Figure 1 connects macro level consumer culture

‑
theories with micro level psychological processes. In respect to CCT, Thompson (2024, p. 565)
has advocated for "ontological reconfigurations of established theorizations," which is exactly

‑
what we set out to accomplish. We attempt to reframe reviewing as a value creating process for

‑
movie consumers, not just as predictive outputs for box office forecasting (e.g., Chintagunta et
al., 2010; Moon et al., 2010). Our findings show that a reviewing practice is able to provide
movie consumers personal value, which shifts the focus of art marketing research from financial
outcomes to experiential outcomes.


Our thesis also addresses Schneider et al.’s (2021, p. 598) observation that even though “the APE
model has been cited over 2,000 times in the last ten years” there has been no empirical study (to
our knowledge) that has applied the model to primary data collected from movie consumers.
While we acknowledge movie consumption experiences are a narrow subsection of the entirety
of consumer evaluations, this thesis still contributes to popularizing the use of the APE model in
art marketing research.


Lastly, this thesis confirms the importance of memory reconstruction in understanding art
consumption experience. While memory theory has been studied extensively in both psychology
and marketing (Jonas et al., 2023), we demonstrate its specific mechanisms in movie
consumption experiences; mainly rewriting significance, temporal anchoring, and humorous
encoding. While our findings are initial and quite heavily dependent on the theoretical
framework, they still warrant further academic investigation.

### **Practical Implications**


In our set-out research aims we theorized how the knowledge we generate could benefit different
stakeholders, such as online review platforms. From our findings, we confirmed the importance
of co-creation when it comes to consumer ideologies and marketplace cultures (Arnould &
Thompson, 2005). These aspects are crucial to the success of online review platforms, since they


35


evoke activity within the user base. Therefore, encouraging the use of reflective writing features

‑ ‑
(e.g., prompts, personal year in review summaries, private notes) may deepen user engagement
more than aggregated star ratings.


Movie marketers can also benefit from considering how user reviews have the capacity to affect
consumption experiences by creating personal value through evaluation (as theorized by
Moliner-Tena et al., 2023). Dedicated movie reviewers consider reviewing as part of the
consumption experience itself and have a highly developed sense of their reviewer identity. This
relates to how consumption choices are made, since the development of propositional reasoning
creates a stronger sense of personal tastes. Hence, movie marketers can encourage an active
reviewing practice to increase the personal investment of movie consumers, who in turn are more
vocal about their opinions (leading to financial outcomes).


Lastly, cultural institutions can benefit from the knowledge of how written reviews (even short
ones) can transform passive fleeting consumption experiences into actualized personal
connections. By developing the propositional reasoning prowess of consumers through
reviewing, cultural institutions can further promote aesthetic literacy of the public.

### **Limitations and Future Research**


There are several limitations that have to be considered when interpreting our findings, such as
our focus on one platform and only on one art form. While there are many forms of art and
online review platforms, we studied only movie reviewing in the context of Letterboxd. Future
research can compare reviewing practices on other prevalent platforms or across art forms (e.g.,
music, literature, gaming).


We also employed a singular perspective, as we focused only on the act of reviewing, not the
effect of reviews on readers. This is a significant limitation as in art consumers can be
significantly influenced by the diaspora of different previous opinions. Future studies could
investigate how reading reviews alters consumption experiences (e.g., reviewing vs.
non‑reviewing audiences).


Our experiential findings are completely qualitative in nature and are, therefore, not quantifiable

‑
or generalizable. Mixed methods research could aim to measure correlations between reviewing
frequency, memory retention, as well as use big data to quantify more specific consumer

sentiments.


36


We acknowledge that the model we created makes the assumption that reviewing is
transformative for all engaged reviewers and that our sample skewed toward highly engaged
users. It is possible that for a significant subset of movie consumers, reviewing produces no
transformation, and our created model does not account for such cases. Future research can

furthermore explore what distinguishes reviewers who experience transformational outcomes

from those who do not.


Our findings could have also been enhanced if our thesis employed a longitudinal research
design. We only studied reviewers who already had some sort of a Letterboxd review practice,
which did not allow us to examine how starting a review practice changes how movie
consumption experiences. A future study could employ a longitudinal research design, wherein
participants are recruited to keep a review practice for some span of time. The results can then
better ascertain whether starting a reviewing practice changes consumption experience.

## Conclusion


This thesis’ aim was to understand how the practice of online reviewing affects movie
consumption experiences. Through netnographic analysis of 260 Letterboxd reviews and
semi-structured interviews with 13 active users, three transformational outcomes were discussed.


First, reviewing extends and deepens the consumption experience. Across participants, the act of
writing a review prolonged engagement with the film, transforming passive viewing into
personal meaning-making. This deepening occurred through affective (emotional catharsis and
closure) and cognitive (forced reflection and over-analysis) prolongation of the movie
experience. The valence effect of reviewing on the movie consumption experience was varied, as
some reviewers reported intensified enjoyment, while others found that further critical thinking
diminished initial positive reactions.


Second, reviewing forces the reconstruction of memory and meaning. Reviews have the ability
to function as temporal anchors, allowing reviewers to revisit a movie experience, as well as
their past selves at specific moments in time. Reviewed films are generally remembered better
than unreviewed ones, but more emotionally vivid reviews might induce memory decay due to
interference (Moliner-Tena et al., 2023).


Third, online movie reviewing performs and affects consumer identities and ideologies.
Performances range from seeking authenticity and originality within the community to rejecting
the community entirely, performing comedy for a generic audience, or asserting privacy as a


37


deliberate positioning strategy. These individual performances collectively constitute and
reproduce the marketplace culture of Letterboxd.


Returning to the central research question, this thesis demonstrates that the act of reviewing
alters the art consumption experience in three interrelated ways: it prolongs engagement, affects
memories of consumption, and performs/shapes identity. These aspects of online reviewing
operate simultaneously and reinforce one another, as a reviewer who writes to process emotions
also inevitably reshapes their memory of the film and performs a version of their cinephile self

for an online audience.


Consequently, our analysis gives support to Arnould & Thompson’s (2015, p. 15) claim that “the
theoretical pairing of commerce and culture” offers “distinctive contributions to the broader
interdisciplinary conversation concerning consumption and society.” Reviewers described
reviews as a constitutive part of how they consumed movies and this suggests that art
consumption is not complete after the viewing experience. Art consumption requires articulation
and, therefore, reviewing is not an accessory to art consumption but a constitutive part of it.
