**Abstract**

This paper explores decision-making processes in experience goods markets,
emphasizing how agents learn to choose in situations where value is uncertain until
after consumption. The study examines agents employing individual and social
learning strategies within a multi-dimensional reinforcement learning framework,
particularly in scenarios of repeated choices. Agents in our model gather insights
from personal experiences and through recommendations within their social networks. The simulation results highlight the benefits of combining individual and
social learning. While social learning yields consistent outcomes across agents, individual learning offers the possibility of higher rewards but also greater risks. A notable finding of this research is the development of asymmetrical influence patterns in
social networks. This phenomenon refers to a tendency where certain agents become
disproportionately influential in guiding others’ choices, leading to a centralization
in how advice is sought and followed within the network. This aspect of the model
sheds light on the nuances of social dynamics in decision-making processes. The
study enhances our understanding of consumer behavior in markets for experience
goods, providing insights into the complex interplay of individual experiences and
social influences in shaping economic decisions.

Economic environments often require agents to make decisions in the face of
uncertainty. Choosing optimally in these circumstances poses a significant challenge, and agents may adopt various strategies to cope. In instances where the
situation occurs only once, opportunities for learning from experience are minimal. However, in contexts where agents repeatedly encounter uncertainty, it is
reasonable to assume that they will engage in a learning process. This process
encompasses not only the strategies employed to acquire knowledge—such as

To make our point clearer, let’s consider the example of experience goods. These
goods are unique because their intrinsic value cannot be assessed until after they
have been consumed. Consequently, when an agent faces the decision to purchase
an experience good, there is inherent uncertainty regarding the optimal choice. In a
scenario involving repeated purchases, while the consumer may opt for the familiar
item, thereby knowing its expected value, the agent remains unaware of potentially
better alternatives that may be available. This situation is often referred as a multiarmed bandit. However, as the details of our model will point out later, the situation
described is slightly different in the way agents behave.
Therefore, there is a trade-off between choosing a known option and exploring
unknown alternatives that might lead to greater utility than the familiar choice. The
assumption of this work is that in such scenarios, individuals are learning not only
to select the right option from a pool of alternatives but are also discerning which
decision-making method yields the most utility. Consider the example of choosing
a restaurant. In large enough cities, for any ranges of budget, a consumer will be
faced with many alternatives, and thus price cannot be the sole decisive factor guiding choice. There is no definitive method for making the best choice under these
circumstances, and people might employ various approaches: selecting the nearest,
choosing the first one that appears in an internet search, or asking acquaintances for
recommendations, to name a few. Then, once a restaurant is chosen, the consumer
evaluates the service based on the utility received. This feedback prompts two questions: Am I satisfied enough to come back? And do I believe that the method I used
to choose this restaurant was the most effective? These two questions initiate two
learning processes: one about finding the best restaurant and the other about determining the most effective method to find the best restaurant.
Three foundational assumptions underpin the model presented: Interactions are
without prices and without strategic games and are conducted by individuals with
bounded rationality (Simon 1955).
As discussed, many economic scenarios cannot be resolved through pricing
mechanisms alone, particularly when alternatives are priced similarly. This situation is common for many experience goods, such as cultural products (books, cinema tickets, records), education, healthcare, or restaurants within the same market

segment.
By ‘interactions without games,’ we mean situations where the agents, in this
context consumers, have no incentive to deceive or engage in behavior that would
diminish the utility of others. This scenario aligns with many experience goods
due to the inherent nature of these products. An interaction between two consumers typically involves one recommending a product to the other, based on their own
experience. Consequently, there is little to no incentive to recommend anything but
the best option, as the recommending agent has already consumed the good. Furthermore, in scenarios of repeated consumption, there usually exists a delay before
the good is consumed again. Thus, consumers recommend restaurants because they
have already patronized them and do not foresee an immediate need to do so again.

The bounded rationality of our agents is manifested in the way they make decisions and, consequently, how they learn within this model. We propose that agents
utilize reinforcement learning in this environment. Reinforcement learning embodies
the ‘Law of Effect’ (Thorndike 1927), which posits that behaviors yielding favorable outcomes are likely to be repeated in the future, whereas those with detrimental
outcomes are avoided. In our scenario, agents begin with no prior knowledge about
the available alternatives and gradually learn to identify the optimal behavior (in this
case, a consumption choice) over time. They improve by being afforded opportunities to repeat choices, thereby exploring different alternatives. This form of learning
has been extensively examined by the behaviorist school of psychology. The significance of reinforcement in non-conscious learning has been established, notably by
Thorndike (1927) and Skinner (1965), for both animals and humans. Unlike conscious learning, where individuals are aware of the learning process, reinforcement
learning occurs without conscious awareness that one’s actions are being influenced
by past reinforcements. While humans are capable of various conscious learning
mechanisms, these processes are cognitively demanding, and many of our actions
are still largely governed by reinforcement learning (Brenner 2006).
In the field of economics, reinforcement learning has primarily been applied in
the context of game theory (Erev and Roth 1998) and to explore social dilemmas
and cooperation (Macy and Flache 2002). Significant contributions that incorporate
this assumption about agents’ economic behaviors include the work of Cross (2008),
1991), who
who develops a model in which firms are depicted as learners; Arthur (
uses a similar principle to devise economic agents that act like human agents’; and
Kirman and Vriend (2000
), who models the Marseille fish market with both buyers
and sellers engaging in reinforcement learning.
As previously mentioned, our objective extends beyond merely observing the outcomes when agents are endowed with a learning mechanism to select from a range
of options. We are equally interested in exploring the consequences of agents learning about their decision-making methods.
To this end, we introduce an alternative method for selection: social learning.
Social learning entails situating the agents within a network that captures their social
interactions.
More specifically, agents will have the opportunity to select a good based on the
recommendations of direct neighbors-essentially, their acquaintances-during each
iteration.

Social learning in the literature mainly involves DeGroot learning or Bayesian
learning (see Golub and Sadler 2017 and Bikhchandani et al. 2021 for extensive and
recent literature reviews). Both methods differ from those we propose here. DeGroot
learning involves summing information from plural neighbors, and Bayesian learning updating prior beliefs, which therefore imply having an initial belief to begin
with. By contrast in the scenario we study here, we define social learning as following the recommendation of one neighbor at a time.

various fields starting from the middle of the twentieth century with the work
of Katz and Lazarsfeld (2017

All of these different approaches involve one agent’s decisions, behavior, or utility

Our model will then approach the case of repeated choice in experience markets
when agents are allowed to use either their own learning mechanism, which will be
modeled as reinforcement learning, or through the use of their social networks. They
then also learn, again through a reinforcement mechanism, about which method yields
the most utility. There are thus two layers of reinforcement learning.
Therefore, we place agents on a network structure and allow them to interact
with their neighbors to make repeated choices in experience goods market. Through
repeated choices and learning from experience, agents learn which products are the
best to pick. Our model also allows agents to choose between individual and social
learning, allowing them to explore the benefits of both approaches and learn about how
to learn in general. By combining these two types of learning, our model offers a novel
approach to studying consumer decision making in markets with multiple similarly
priced options.
The simulations of the model described below give results about whether individual
or social learning is preferable and about the naturally emerging proportion of each
type of learners. We also explore the role of curiosity and trust for optimal decisions
both at individual and population levels.

**2 The model**


**2.1 Agents and products**


We treat a fixed population of _n_ agents each of which is repeatedly choosing
which of 10 products to consume. Consumption lasts one period so at each period
every agent repeats the decision. While the 10 alternatives all cater to the same
needs, they don’t all produce the same payoff/amount of satisfaction. Each product is associated with a given payoff, which remains the same for the whole simulation. Payoffs vary on a discrete scale from 1 to 10, and we refer to products by
the level of payoffs they reward. (So product 6 gives a payoff of 6 to the agent
for example.) Tastes are homogeneous so each product delivers the same payoff
for everyone in the population and everyone prefers product _i_ to product _i_ − 1 .
Initially, agents have no information on the utility each product delivers; they
only know that there are 10 products. Consequently, at the start of the simulation,
agents have no way of discerning which product could give the best payoff.


**2.2 Interaction**


Agents of our population will be able to interact among themselves. By interaction, we mean asking or giving advice on which product to choose for the current stage of the simulation. When giving advice, the agent will simply tell which
product it would have chosen for the current round. Agents always answer solicitations from other agents. An agent who receive advice always follows it.
The interaction process takes place on a network structure that we define as
follows. We use an undirected graph that represents the ties between individuals.
The nature of these ties captures the possibility for any of two connected agents
to advise on buying a good or service. So this type of tie can involve friendship,
family, or colleagues but it might be harder to exist between an employer and its
employee for example.
Agents are embedded in a social network where each agent has a set of direct
neighbors with whom they can interact. These neighbors represent individuals
they are connected to. Interaction occurs only between directly connected agents,
meaning that information or recommendations are exchanged exclusively within
these local connections.

To keep a coherent interaction scheme, advice always comes from someone
who is, at least for the current round, using its own experience to choose a good.
We want to avoid a situation where _i_ asks neighbor _j_ about which alternative to
choose, _j_ answers product _A_, but itself asks _k_ and, following _k_ ’s advice, chooses
product _B_ . Instead, if _i_ asks _j_, and _j_ itself asks _k_, and _k_ is an individual learner,
then _k_ ’s advice flows back to both _j_ and _i_ . The rare but problematic case where _i_
asks _j_, who asks _k_, who itself asks _i_ (creating a cycle) is resolved by forcing one
among the three to become an individual learner for the round.

over time based on past experiences.

moment, which is determined by a probability vector. Thus, when an agent _i_ is asked to
recommend a good to _j_, it draws a good using the probabilities in its vector and gives
the result to _j_ . This vector is continually adjusted through reinforcement, ensuring that
it represents the agent’s up-to-date preferences. It is this dynamic aspect of the recommendation system that facilitates the spread of the most advantageous alternatives.

It explains why, while the two sub-populations reach equivalent average aggregate payoffs (around 1340 for both), individual learning can be seen as a riskier

**3.3 Curiosity**


We have so far assumed a certain level of ‘curiosity’ in our agents. The issue they
face—navigating a pool of alternatives with unknown values—necessarily involves
a trade-off between exploration and exploitation. This means agents must balance the search for better options against the choice to settle for an alternative that
appears to be satisfactory.
In this framework, ‘curiosity’ refers to the duration dedicated to exploring unfamiliar alternatives as opposed to exploiting those already known. This includes
selecting previously untried goods and reconsidering goods that didn’t initially seem
optimal, even after encountering better options.

**3.4 Trust‑based social interactions**


So far, we have only considered unweighted network interactions, where a social
learner randomly selects a neighbor for advice with equal probability. In our model,
agents not only learn which products yield the highest payoffs but also adapt their
decision-making method, choosing between individual learning and social learning. However, in the current framework, social learners do not distinguish between
neighbors when seeking advice.
To extend this model, we introduce a third layer of reinforcement learning. In
addition to learning about products and the choice between individual and social
learning, agents now also learn which neighbors provide the best advice. This means
that social learners will reinforce the probability of consulting neighbors who have
previously given them high-reward recommendations. Over time, this adaptive


Experience goods, reinforcement learning, and social networks﻿ 1035


process could lead to a restructuring of network interactions, where certain agents
become preferred sources of advice.
This additional layer of learning raises two key questions: (1) Will it improve
decision-making outcomes for social learners? (2) How will the structure of network interactions evolve as some connections become more frequently utilized than
others?

The process by which agents learn to identify which neighbors to consult follows the same reinforcement learning approach used for product selection and learning method selection. Specifically, after receiving advice from a neighbor _j_, agent _i_
adjusts the probability of consulting _j_ in future rounds based on the payoff received.
Good advice increases this probability, while poor advice has a minimal effect,
ensuring that over time, agents rely more on consistently helpful neighbors.
While agents cannot create or remove ties, this adaptive mechanism alters the
_effective structure_ of the network. Initially, interactions occur on an _undirected_
network where all connections are equally likely to be used. Over time, as agents
reinforce preferences for certain neighbors, the probability of selecting each neighbor becomes asymmetric. This effectively transforms the network into a _weighted,_
_directed graph_, where the weight of a directed edge from agent _i_ to agent _j_ corresponds to the likelihood that _i_ will seek advice from _j_ . A weight of 0 implies that
_i_ never consults _j_, while a weight of 1 means _i_ always follows _j_ ’s recommendations. The nodes and ties remain the same, but when viewed through the lens of each
agent’s probability of asking advice from its neighbors, the network can be considered directed (since _i_ ’s probability of asking _j_ is generally different from _j_ ’s probability of asking _i_ ) and weighted (as these probabilities range from 0 to 1).
This process can be interpreted as a _form of trust formation_, where reinforcement
learning leads agents to increasingly favor certain sources of information. As previously discussed, reinforcement learning can result in _lock-in_ to a particular alternative. In this case, the same mechanism can lead an agent to exclusively rely on a specific neighbor, even if that neighbor later changes its advice. This dynamic naturally
reshapes the _flow of information_ within the network, concentrating influence among
certain agents.

**4 Conclusion**


Modeling agents’ behavior in the context of experience goods—where consumers
learn through repeated choices without prior information—presents challenges, as
individuals rely on adaptive heuristics rather than fixed optimization. Our findings
suggest that reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for this process,
capturing both individual and social learning dynamics. In particular, we show that
social learning significantly improves aggregate outcomes, even when no agent is
initially better informed. This aligns with empirical evidence from experimental
economics demonstrating that individuals combine personal reinforcement learning with social information when navigating uncertainty (Fudenberg et al. 2012).
In networked environments, agents often imitate successful peers, reinforcing the
importance of advice structures in shaping economic behavior (Dal Bó and Fréchette 2018). These results highlight that boundedly rational agents learn through

trial-and-error, yet do so in a way that is systematically influenced by their social
context.

Beyond individual decision-making, our model also shows that social learning
fosters the emergence of influential network hubs, as certain agents become disproportionately consulted for advice. This mirrors empirical findings from network economics: In organizational settings, informal advice networks tend to evolve toward
structures where a few central individuals act as knowledge brokers (Aral and Van
Alstyne 2011). Similarly, field studies show that economic choices such as financial
adoption or insurance uptake spread more effectively when channeled through highcentrality individuals (Cai et al. 2015). These results confirm that social learning
does not merely accelerate knowledge diffusion—it also reshapes the structure of
economic interactions, leading to persistent centralization effects in advice-seeking
networks.

This centralization, however, raises an important question: does social learning
always benefit the group as a whole? While our results indicate that social learners
outperform individual learners on average, this does not preclude inefficiencies in
information aggregation. In our model, agents rely on reinforcement mechanisms to
weigh the trustworthiness of different sources, but in real-world settings, informational cascades and echo chambers could emerge, leading to inefficient equilibria. If
certain agents become central not because they provide the best advice but simply
because they were consulted early on, the long-run efficiency of the system could
be compromised. Similar concerns have been raised in empirical work on financial
and political networks, where early movers gain disproportionate influence regardless of actual expertise (Banerjee et al. 2013). Future work could explore whether
trust reinforcement in social learning systematically converges to optimal outcomes
or whether suboptimal centrality effects persist even when better information exists
elsewhere in the network.
Additionally, our findings contribute to broader discussions in market learning
and firm behavior. While the model focuses on individual consumers, the learning
mechanisms we explore are directly applicable to firms operating under uncertainty.
Experimental research on adaptive pricing and Cournot competition shows that
firms using reinforcement learning mechanisms-adjusting strategies based on past
profits rather than full rational expectations-tend to converge to competitive market
outcomes over time (Huck et al. 2004). However, the process of reaching equilibrium is shaped by the availability of social information: when firms can observe successful competitors, they tend to adapt faster and avoid prolonged inefficient learning periods (Offerman et al. 2002). This suggests that social learning mechanisms
not only help individual agents but could also lead to less time elapsed before market efficiency materializes.
By integrating a third layer of reinforcement-allowing agents to learn whom to
trust for advice-our model captures an essential mechanism of real-world economic
learning: the development of selective trust in information sources. This process is
fundamental to financial decision-making, labor market referrals, and technology
adoption, where individuals rely on a mix of individual experience, social influence, and strategic trust-building. While our model assumes that agents act myopically when updating their trust in others, future research could incorporate strategic

incentives—where certain agents deliberately manipulate advice to benefit themselves, a phenomenon observed in real-world settings such as financial markets
(Crawford and Sobel 1982).
More broadly, this research contributes to a growing literature on how social
interactions shape economic outcomes. By formalizing reinforcement-based social
learning in a network setting, we provide a framework that bridges behavioral economics, network theory, and market learning models. The results indicate that even
in the absence of perfect information or rational expectations, decentralized learning
processes can still lead to efficient information diffusion. However, the potential for
network-driven inequalities, misallocation of influence, and path-dependent inefficiencies highlights the need for further empirical validation and refinement of these
models. Future work could extend this framework by incorporating heterogeneous
preferences, evolving product quality, or strategic incentives in advice-sharing-all
critical dimensions of learning in economic environments shaped by both individual
and collective adaptation.


**Appendix 1: Simulation procedure**


**Model definitions**


Three models are tested in this study:


- **Model 1 (No Social Learning)** : Agents individually learn which product to
select using a reinforcement learning mechanism.

- **Model 2 (Individual and Social Learning)** : Agents can either learn individually or ask a randomly selected neighbor for advice.

- **Model 3 (Individual, Social, and Neighbor Learning)** : Social learners select
which neighbor to ask based on past success, using a reinforcement learning
mechanism.


Results are first presented in the paper for Models 1 and 2, then for Model 3.


**Simulation framework**


All three models follow a common simulation structure, where agents make decisions over multiple rounds. The key probabilities governing individual learning,
product selection, and neighbor selection (in Model 3) evolve through a reinforcement learning mechanism based on the observed payoffs of selected products. The
details of this learning process are discussed in the main paper.


**Simulation steps**


At each time step _t_, the following sequence of events occurs:


Experience goods, reinforcement learning, and social networks﻿ 1041


1. **Initialization** :


  - A population of _n_ agents is created.

  - Agents are placed on an Erdös–Rényi random network with a connectivity
density of 10%.

  - Each agent is assigned initial probability distributions (uniform) for:


– Product selection.

– Choosing between individual and social learning.
– (In Model 3) Selecting a neighbor when using social learning.


  - The reinforcement learning parameter _휈_ is set, along with the number of
rounds.


2. **Agent Selection and Decision-Making** :


  - An agent _i_ is randomly selected.

  - The agent chooses between **individual learning** and **social learning** :


– The choice follows a probability distribution that evolves through reinforcement learning.


3. **If the agent chooses individual learning** :


  - The agent selects a product based on its current probability distribution.

  The agent observes the product’s payoff.

  - The agent updates:


– The probability of selecting this product again (and thus of other products).
– The probability of choosing individual learning in future rounds (and
thus of choosing social learning).


4. **If the agent chooses social learning** :


  - **(Model 2)** The agent selects a random neighbor.

  - **(Model 3)** The agent selects a neighbor based on a probability distribution
that evolves over time.

  - The neighbor recommends a product.

  - The agent picks the recommended product.

  The agent observes the product’s payoff.

  - The agent updates:


– The probability of selecting this product again.
– The probability of choosing social learning in future rounds.
– **(Model 3)** The probability of asking the same neighbor again.


5. **Simulation Continuation** :


  The process repeats until the predefined number of rounds is reached.
