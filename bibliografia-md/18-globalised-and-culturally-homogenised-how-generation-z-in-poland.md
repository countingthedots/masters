**ABSTRACT**
Globalisation and cultural homogenisation are still vital processes that
drive the unification of leisure behaviour around the world, especially
among the youngest generation. This is particularly important for postsocialist countries because until 1989, there was no access to Western
ways of spending free time. Polish Generation Z is the first generation
born and raised entirely in a free country. Therefore, the question arises
whether the 30 years that have passed since the fall of the Berlin Wall have
been sufficient to unify Generation Z’s leisure time behaviour under
globalisation’s influence. The study aimed to determine the leisure beha­
viour of Generation Z in Poland in the context of cultural homogenisation.
The analysis includes 1153 surveys with representatives of Generation
Z from Polish cities. The study showed that the structure of Polish
Generation Z’s leisure activities is dominated by multimedia entertain­
ment: using the Internet, listening to music, watching YouTube or VOD
(Video On Demand) platforms. Globalisation processes leading to pro­
gressive cultural homogenisation contribute to the spread of similar forms
of spending free time originating in the so-called Western culture, which is
also visible in Poland. Practical implications are presented for policy­
makers responsible for creating directions for youth leisure activities in
cities.

Generation Z could not be considered as a global generation without the influence of cultural
homogenisation. Santos (2004) indicates that cultural homogenisation is a process that reduces
cultural diversity by popularising uniform cultural symbols, including leisure activities. For many
researchers, it is cultural dynamics that are fundamental to the process of globalisation (Cicchelli &
Octobre, 2018; Rubdy & Alsagoff, 2014). Moreover, globalisation is strongly linked to cultural
homogenisation – both processes strongly influence each other, increasing the strength of their
interactions (Hopper, 2007; Jennings, 2010).

This article focuses on cultural homogenisation in relation to one of the former Eastern
Bloc countries (Poland). In the case of this region of the world, it should be stressed that
Westernisation understood as the acceptance of Western culture began in 1989 after the fall of
the Berlin Wall. The changes initiated at that time, which accelerated the process of globalisa­
tion, had a powerful impact on the transformation of youth’s leisure time behaviour. The
youth transition phenomena that started after 1989 concerned all spheres of life; through the

countries based on the available literature.


**Theoretical background**


Leisure time should be considered from many different aspects for the development of each
person and the entire society. For example, a number of literature items indicate that leisure is
most important for children and young people. Through leisure activities children and youths
socialise (Sichling & Plöger, 2018) and form their identities (Codina et al., 2017). Its impor­
tance is also shown by the number of disciplines dealing with this subject including economics
(Nazareth, 2007; Smith, 2013), psychology (Ajzen & Driver, 1991; Codina et al., 2017),
physical culture and health sciences (Bednall et al., 2012; Birkeland et al., 2009), sociology
(Bittman & Wajcman, 2000; Mullan, 2009) and geography (Zhu et al., 2010; Mowl, 2014;
Glover, 2017).

Geographically speaking, it is extremely interesting to consider leisure time in the context of
cultural homogenisation. This process refers to the reduction of cultural diversity through the
dissemination of various cultural symbols, including customs, ideas and values (Barker, 2003;
Jennings, 2010). There are two different approaches to this process. The first one understands
cultural homogenisation as the acceptance of Western culture around the world along with life­
styles, consumption patterns, leisure patterns or ideas and beliefs (Conversi, 2010; Crothers, 2017).
Nevertheless, the level and scale of acceptance of Western culture by individual nations vary greatly.
Depending on the level of socio-economic development and local specifics, there are many types of
cultural homogenisation understood as Westernisation (Pingali, 2007), Americanisation (Stephan,
2006), or McDonaldization (Ritzer & Malone, 2000). This type of cultural homogenisation can
manifest itself in the process of homogenising the content provided by television, as Gordon (2009)
describes to Jamaica’s television industry: ‘through expanding media globalization, content homo­
genization emerges in countries such as Jamaica with the indirect flow of ideas about program pro­
duction and programming strategy through satellite from places such as the United States’ (Gordon,
2009, p. 317). As a result of a process similar to that observed in Jamaica, the same types of
programmes such as reality shows, comedy, etc., can be seen on television in various countries
worldwide (Fung, 2015). However, in most countries, the unification of particular social or
economic behaviours adopted from the U.S. culture occurs with the proliferation of companies
such as McDonald’s Corporation, Nike, Starbucks, Gap, KFC (Robertson & White, 2007).

A second approach to cultural homogenisation is not so much about the domination of Western
culture as about a mixture of different cultures, without an advantage of any one of them (Holton,
2000; Robertson & White, 2007). One can also follow Murray and Overton (2015) in considering
that: ‘contemporary globalization has led to the hybridization of culture to a greater degree than
ever before’ (p. 259). Simultaneously, a very strong trend of a reassertion of local culture as
a response to homogenisation is noticeable (Islam et al., 2019; Van Der Bly, 2007). An example
of such hybridisation is the interesting case study of a small town of 15,000 people in Ireland,
Leixlip. As Van Der Bly (2007) describes, Leixlip was integrated into a global economic network
through Intel and Hewlett Packard’s investment in large manufacturing facilities. The author
identifies economic globalisation as the dominant globalisation in the presented case study.
However, Van Der Bly (2007) also points out that right after economic globalisation came cultural
globalisation, which translates into the adoption of new global patterns by local people. The results
indicate that cultural globalisation supports the development of heterogeneity ‘but within the
context of one world culture, namely as local adaptations of world cultural forms’ (Van Der Bly,
2007, p. 234).

This adaptation of global services and products to local socio-cultural conditions, that is,
glocalisation, can also be studied in youth leisure behaviour.


_**Generational differences in leisure**_


However, it is impossible to talk about the characteristic features of Generation Z in spending
leisure time without comparing them to the characteristics of previous cohorts. At the same time,
the distinction of generations by researchers on the basis of age and common experiences was
created in reference to Karl Mannheim’s works and generational theory from the first half of the
20th century. Although the boundaries between generations are often blurred, distinguishing
generational differences or establishing specific characteristics for particular cohorts is of great
importance in social sciences (Goh & Lee, 2018) and economics (Dabija et al., 2020), e.g. for
marketing purposes (Williams & Page, 2011). Nowadays, apart from Generation Z, the most
important cohorts are Baby Boomers, Generation X, and Generation Y (see Bejtkovský, 2016).
Concerning these generations, differences in the leisure time behaviour of Generation Z can be
recognised. Generation Z is currently the youngest identified cohort.

These young people are mainly people born after 1994. However, the indicated boundary is not
a rigid point in time. McCrindle (2014) points out that ‘as birth rates picked up in 1995, we had the
beginnings of Generation Z. Marketers are tempted to begin a generation at a key year like, say,
2000, but there is no demographic or sociological justification for such date picking’ (p. 12). Thus,
Seemiller and Grace (2018) point to 1995 as the beginning of Generation Z due to the dynamic
development of personal technology. On the other hand, Shirish et al. (2016) distinguish 1990 as the
borderline ‘due to accelerated technological advancements’ (p. 1111), and Pew Research Center
even points to 1997: ‘anyone born between 1981 and 1996 (. . .) is considered a Millennial, and
anyone born from 1997 onward is part of a new generation’ (Dimock, 2019, p. 2). Regardless of the
point in time, it should be assumed that nowadays young people can be identified with Generation
Z. Generation Z is a global generation (Seemiller & Grace, 2018), not knowing reality without the
Internet, freely using it along with various technological innovations (Betz, 2019). This group is
characterised by creativity and innovation (Dabija et al., 2019; Seemiller & Grace, 2018), and
significant activity and proficiency in the social media world (Bejtkovský, 2016). Another of their
distinguishing features is multitasking, as representatives of this generation do several things at
once; this applies mainly to multimedia, e.g. watching TV, playing games, and sending text
messages (Kivunja, 2014; Seemiller & Grace, 2018).

The feature most often indicated by researchers distinguishing Generation Z from other gen­
erations is a solid attachment to new technologies. This has a direct impact on differences in leisure
time activities. It is generally accepted that the older people get, the lower percentage of them use

digital technologies (Vogels, 2019). This is not only true for Western countries – this regularity is
also observed in Middle Eastern countries (Sivan & Cohen-Gewerc, 2018; Zhitomirsky-Geffet &
Blau, 2016), Central and Eastern Europe (Kalmus et al., 2013), classified as developing countries
(Celik & Schoreels, 2014), and China (Zhou et al., 2014). There are also new risks associated with
attachment to new technologies in the form of smartphone addiction. A study conducted on three
generations (X, Y, Z) showed that Generation Z, which is familiar with mobile technology from an
early age, is less prone to addiction than the other generations, who, as it were, ‘overnight’ were
invited into the virtual world (Zhitomirsky-Geffet & Blau, 2016). Moreover, the results of this study
indicated a generational transition in the use of technology. Generation X is most likely to use email
and SMS, Generation Y is most likely to use social media, while Generation Z is most likely to use
Whatsapp (Zhitomirsky-Geffet & Blau, 2016). However, recent literature shows that one of the
most popular apps among the youngest generation is TikTok (e.g. Weimann & Masri, 2020).

In general, younger people are more active online than older people (Celik & Schoreels, 2014;
Helsper, 2010). This is due to the rapid development of digital technologies since the beginning of
the 21st century when the first representatives of Generation Z were still in kindergarten and grew
up with the Internet and smartphones from an early age (Lai & Hong, 2015). Today, smartphone
ownership and Internet access among young adults is commonplace. As indicated by Pew Research
Center, about 95% of U.S. teens own a smartphone, and 45% of them say they use the Internet
almost constantly (Anderson & Jiang, 2018).

However, even greater differences can be seen in the use of other means of telecommunications.
Although television viewing has been one of the most popular and widespread leisure activities for
decades, the generational differences associated with this pastime are now widening. For example,
while Americans on average spend 2.73 hours a day watching television, U.S. youth ages 15 to 24
spend only 2.03 hours. The percentage of people watching television increases with successive
cohorts until it reaches 4 hours per day for Baby Boomers (Seemiller & Grace, 2018). Similar
relationships have been observed in Estonia, where as age increases, individuals surveyed were more
likely to watch television (Kalmus et al., 2013). For Generation Z, ‘watching TV’ is using VOD
(Video On Demand) platforms that allow them to watch any programme at any time (Seemiller &
Grace, 2018). Despite the differences in television viewing frequency, it is indicated that television is
one of the few forms of entertainment that unites all generations (Kalmus et al., 2013).

Another form of leisure related to technology is games. Research from the U.S. shows that 90% of
Generation Z representatives play video games. This is an incomparably higher percentage com­
pared to previous generations (Seemiller & Grace, 2018). Most representatives of Generation X or
older treat games as activities that take place outdoors or as board games. In contrast, Generation
Z overwhelmingly treats games as video games (Çobanoğlu et al., 2018).

Moving away from strictly technology-related activities, reading should also be mentioned. It is
indicated that older generations read books and newspapers more frequently and willingly than
younger people (Twenge, 2009), including Generation Z (Voorveld & Van der Goot, 2013).
Adolescents do not rate reading books as an exciting and preferred activity (Merga, 2016). One
of the reasons cited for this is the increased interest in other leisure activities, such as Internet use,
social media, and video games (Majid, 2018; Merga, 2016).

Besides sedentary leisure activities, generational differences can also be observed in prosocial
behaviour. On the one hand, researchers like Sivan and Cohen-Gewerc (2018) point to a trend in
which the youngest generation is most engaged in social issues. This approach is also supported by
other research findings, which show that in addition to those aged 65–74, young people aged 16–24
are the most likely to volunteer (Nichols & Ralston, 2016). On the other hand, an argument has been
made about increasing narcissism and individualism among the youngest generations, which
manifests in less social engagement (Hustinx et al., 2010; Twenge et al., 2012). It is difficult to
identify a homogeneous trend in the adoption of social behaviours. However, Jardim and Marques

_**Cultural homogenisation and Generation Z’s leisure time**_


Geographically based analyses of differences and similarities are essential in the issue of cultural
homogenisation. One of the broader and more important papers was written by Rees (2017); it
analyses the similarities and differences in the daily activities of children aged 12 in 16 different
countries. The most important conclusion of the study is that the differences in the duration of each
activity between countries in Europe are small (Rees, 2017). Studies from Spain show that the main
forms of leisure activities for Generation Z are physical recreation and digital entertainment with
little volunteer and civic involvement (Caballo Villar et al., 2017). Data on the Hungarian lifestyle of
Generation Z also indicate that, in comparison with previous generations, electronic media are
gaining importance among young people – there is a decline in interest in television in favour of the
broadly understood Internet (Nagy et al., 2017). However, spending free time online carries serious
risks. Makaruk and Wójcik (2013) stress that in Poland 13% of teenagers are dysfunctional Internet
users (this term includes both the abuse of the Internet and the threat of abuse, i.e. showing only
certain symptoms). This is due, among other things, to the replacement of the real playground with
virtual playgrounds, including video games (Brooks et al., 2016). A comparative analysis of the
percentage of young people addicted to the Internet shows that it reaches 20% in Asian countries
(South Korea – 20.3%, Taiwan – 17.9%, Qatar – 17.3%, China – 13.5%), 18% in Europe (UK –
18.3%, Greece – 15.3%, Italy – 5.4%, Croatia – 3.4%), and 25% in the USA (Mihajlov & Vejmelka,
2017). Another study even indicates that as much as 78% of American teenagers are addicted to
smartphones and the Internet (Csobanka, 2016).

All these interesting research results from various countries confirm that in the dimension of free
time one can speak of globalisation and cultural homogenisation. These processes manifest them­
selves in similar forms of spending leisure time (Gronow & Southerton, 2009) and the spread of the
leisure-oriented lifestyle (Reid & Mannell, 1994). They acquire a special meaning in the context of
the global character of Generation Z, as indicated by one of the representatives of this generation in
the work of Seemiller and Grace (2018): ‘our generation will change the world by working to
establish, maintain, and respect a globalized, multicultural community (. . .)’ (p. 251).

In conclusion, it seems that Generation Z’s leisure behaviour can be a good indicator of
globalisation and cultural homogenisation processes. When considering how Generation
Z spends its free time, the influence of global carriers of culture, i.e. the consumption of films or
music, is taken into account (Cicchelli & Octobre, 2018).

Generally, there is a lack of research that captures the changes occurring in different areas of
post-socialist society within the framework of cultural homogenisation. Moreover, less attention
has been paid to the cultural homogenisation of Polish youth, and more specifically to checking
whether Polish youth have unified their leisure behaviour. Hence, the aim of the study was to
determine the leisure behaviour of the first generation born and raised entirely in free Poland. The
paper presents a new and relevant approach. Firstly, a broad spectrum of leisure time activities is
considered in opposition to the residual leisure time research (concerning watching TV or reading
books). Secondly, leisure studies have been barely contextualised in cultural homogenisation, so the
study was theoretically framed within this concept.

In order to identify the leisure behaviour of Generation Z in Poland in the context of cultural
homogenisation, the following questions were asked: what forms of spending free time does
Generation Z in Poland undertake and how often? Against a backdrop of the growing role of the
Internet among youth, another question is: how much time does Polish Generation Z spend online

**Methods**


_**Procedure**_


In this study, in order to determine the leisure behaviour of Generation Z in Poland, research was
conducted in selected Polish cities. In the first stage, a group of cities was analysed in terms of socioeconomic development using the development pattern method (see Miłek, 2018; Rząsa et al., 2019).
The development pattern method is one of the methods in the field of numerical taxonomy (linear
ordering method). This method is used when there is a need to divide objects according to specific
guidelines and to designate classes. This procedure allows us to assess which of the objects is better
(or worse) and compare them with each other. The analysis was based on statistical data obtained
from the Local Data Bank of the Central Statistical Office of Poland (LDB CSO). The data used
referred to the end of 2017. One hundred forty-eight cities for which LDB CSO statistical data were
available were included in the analysis. The database created consisted of nine variables on socioeconomic development of cities: (1) income per capita, (2) availability of supermarkets, (3) share of
working-age people in the total population, (4) natural growth, (5) internal migration balance, (6)
number of entities of national economy registered in the REGON register per 1,000 working-age
people, (7) number of people working per 1,000 inhabitants, (8) share of people at pre-working age
in the total population, (9) number of entities of national economy employing less than 9 people per
10,000 working-age inhabitants. It was assumed that each trait was of equal importance (no weights
were assigned to traits).

The assumption of the development pattern method is the lack of correlation between the
variables. Therefore, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated, and on its basis, a group of
indicators was selected that showed no correlation at 0.05 level. Due to the high correlation
coefficients, the last two variables were excluded from further stages of the study.

In the last phase, three classes of urban development were separated: low, medium and high. One
city was drawn from each class for the research: Suwałki, Oleśnica and Bełchatów. This procedure
made it possible to ensure the representativeness of the sample in terms of socio-economic
conditions.


_**Questionnaire**_


In order to determine the forms in which Generation Z in Polish cities spends free time and how
frequently it does so, the questionnaire was prepared. It was made on the basis of an extensive
literature review and pilot study. For this purpose the main groups of leisure activities were adapted
from the revised Victoria Longitudinal Study (VLS) (Jopp & Hertzog, 2010), as well as from the
previous youth studies (e.g. Opić & Đuranović, 2014; Spilková, 2015). The next step was to conduct
a pilot study in the city of Rumia on a sample of 185 representatives of Generation Z to ensure ease
of understanding and appropriateness of questions. The final questionnaire consisted of 17 forms of
spending leisure time (e.g. watching YouTube, playing computer games, meeting friends, shopping)
divided into 4 groups (entertainment, culture, social life, prosocial behaviour). For each of the
forms, participants indicated the frequency of performing a given activity on a scale from 0 (never)
to 5 (very often). This question also allowed us to determine the amount of time that Generation
Z spends online and to answer the question whether the time spent virtually is reflected in the
reduced interest in other leisure forms.

_**Participants**_


The presented study was of a quantitative nature and was carried out using the survey ques­
tionnaire. It was carried out among representatives of Generation Z in 2018. The study was
designed under ethical guidelines. Before starting the research, consents were obtained from the
Faculty of Earth Sciences and Spatial Management at Nicolaus Copernicus University in Toruń
(Poland) and principals of schools (where the research was conducted). Participants gave
informed consent to take part in the study. The survey was conducted in high schools in selected
cities using printed questionnaires. The respondents filled in the questionnaire anonymously
during school classes. The total sample consisted of 1153 persons (517 from Suwałki, 292 from
Oleśnica and 344 from Bełchatów). Assuming a confidence level of 95%, the maximum error in
the analysed cities did not exceed 5%. The confidence level was calculated for a population aged
15–20 years (Table 1).

The age of the surveyed representatives of Generation Z was between 15 and 20 years, with the
largest group in all three cities being people aged 17 and 18 (46.9% and 36.9% respectively). Women
(60.2%) were more frequent participants in the study than men (39.8%).


_**Data analysis**_


The analysis of quantitative data included elements of descriptive statistics (mean, standard devia­
tion, feature distribution) and the Spearman correlation coefficient.

Descriptive statistics allowed us to determine the frequency and dispersion of undertaking
specific forms of leisure activities. Moreover, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to
indicate the relationship (correlation) between particular ways of spending leisure time.


**Findings**


Taking into account the answers to the question _How often do you undertake the following forms_
_of spending free time?_, it was indicated that representatives of Generation Z were most likely to
spend their free time listening to music (M = 3.67), meeting with friends (3.33) or using social
media (3.09) (Table 2). Less frequent activities included using YouTube (2.82) and VOD services
(2.20), developing one’s interests (2.54) and physical activity (2.50). Out of all the possibilities,
they were the least engaged in prosocial activities (0.92) and visiting theatres, museums, or art
galleries (0.82) – for these activities, the _SD_ assumed the lowest values, which means these
answers were the least varied.

Listening to music was indicated as at least a frequent form of spending free time by the vast
majority of respondents (61.8%). More than half (52.4%) of the respondents meet with friends at
least frequently, while 44.1% use social media as often. On the other hand, as much as 44.1% of the
total number of respondents do not spend their time playing computer games, and almost half do
not visit cultural institutions and do not undertake prosocial behaviour (49.1% and 48.7%, respec­
tively). About half of the respondents rarely or very rarely watch TV (53.1%), read books or press
(49.8%), travel (51.3%), and as much as 62.9% rarely or very rarely spend time in the cinema.

**Discussion and conclusion**


In general, the results of the study provide new remarks into youth’s leisure activities. Our
contribution to the existing knowledge was to contextualise this issue in the framework of cultural
homogenisation and show that this process also affects leisure. Thanks to this study, we have gained
knowledge of what forms of leisure activities are most frequently undertaken by Generation Z in
Poland and to what extent they are similar to their peers in other countries in this regard.

With respect to the main issue raised in the present paper, i.e. forms of spending free time by the
Polish Generation Z, the global characterisation of this cohort as strongly attached to technology
and the Internet has been confirmed. The results indicate that the predominant type of leisure
activities is multimedia entertainment. More specifically, young people in Poland most often listen
to music and use social media, then watch videos on YouTube and VOD. Within this group of
activities, Generation Z in Poland is least likely to watch TV and play computer games. There is
a noticeable cultural homogenisation of Polish Generation Z in the frequency of spending free time
online compared to their peers in Western countries. Codina and Pestana (2017) showed in their
leisure budget survey that Generation Z in Spain spends its leisure time most and longest through
mass media use (mainly including online activities). More than 70% of respondents used this form
at least 5 times a week, most often 1 to 2 hours a day (Codina & Pestana, 2017). Similarly, studies
focusing on children’s (young representatives of Generation Z) time use indicate that Poland,
compared to Western countries (Malta, Norway, UK, Spain), does not show significant changes in
this aspect – spending time in front of a computer dominates (Rees, 2017). In the post-socialist
countries a similar picture of Generation Z is emerging. The use of the Internet and digital
entertainment is the most popular form of spending free time in Hungary (Nagy et al., 2017), the
Czech Republic, Slovakia (Spilková, 2015), Bulgaria, and Kosovo (Glapa et al., 2013). Moreover,
indirectly, the results imply that the role of television as a driver for globalisation has been taken
over by the Internet (see Borcuch et al., 2012).

In terms of leisure activities, it was pointed out that other leisure activities of Generation Z in
Poland are losing their importance at the hands of passive leisure activities related to online
entertainment. This is particularly evident in the small proportion of television viewing time. Ondemand entertainment displaces the need to watch TV programmes which are broadcast at
a predefined time. The exact process has been diagnosed for youth in other countries, as confirmed
by studies from Estonia (Kalmus et al., 2013) or the USA (Seemiller & Grace, 2018). As Kalmus et al.
(2013) point out, young people ‘are the most active in using the Internet . . . Conversely, this
generation is most passive in consuming traditional media’ (p. 22).

Moreover, this conclusion can also be extended to participation in culture – Polish Generation
Z does it very rarely (excluding participation in culture in the form of listening to music). In relation
to culture, the most popular leisure activity is going to the cinema, which Generation Z in Poland
chose relatively rarely in any case. Polish Generation Z reads books very rarely or not at all, and
going to the theatre is in the last place in the leisure time structure. Therefore, once again,
Generation Z’s spending free time in Poland is different from previous generations, and at the
same time, very similar to peer cohorts from other nations. This is confirmed by research conducted
on teenagers from the Netherlands (Voorveld & Van der Goot, 2013), Australia (Merga, 2016), or
Singapore (Majid, 2018), which shows that reading books is considered an unattractive way of
spending free time. As Merga (2016) writes, ‘the most commonly cited reason for adolescent
disengagement in recreational book reading is arguably preference for other recreational pursuits’
(p. 2). Globally young people are less and less likely to undertake this type of activities, as newer,
more engaging, and more attractive ways of participating in the broadly understood culture are
emerging. The participatory culture concept perfectly illustrates this phenomenon – representatives
of Generation Z are no longer just consumers but prosumers. Simultaneously, they are becoming
prosumers of globalisation and cultural homogenisation with the support of social media (Jenkins &
Ito, 2016), including YouTube (Burgess & Green, 2018; Chau, 2010).

As in the case of culture, Polish Generation Z rarely undertakes activities of a voluntary or
prosocial nature. Similar observations have been noted in European research on Generation
Z (Caballo Villar et al., 2017; Nagy et al., 2017; Rees, 2017). While it is indicated that young people
can successfully create social change (Ho et al., 2015), a small percentage of them do so, more often
in their leisure time using social media. However, it is indicated that the use of smartphones and
social media can support social activities of young people (Kim et al., 2016).

To sum up, comparing the results obtained with other research results it should be stated that
Generation Z in Poland differs from its peers to a negligible extent. Globalisation processes leading
to progressive cultural homogenisation contribute to the spread of similar forms of spending free
time originating in the so-called Western culture. This is also noticeable in Poland after 30 years of
systemic transformation. Generation Z as a global generation by nature, born ‘with their brains
wired to the Internet’ is the best example of this phenomenon – in particular, this manifests itself in
the ways of spending leisure time. As Kreisel (2004) points out, free time space is increasingly
moving from the ‘real’ to the ‘virtual’ dimension, which has been confirmed in Polish Generation
Z’s leisure time behaviour. Thus, this is one of the leading platforms of this cohort becoming more
similar to their peers around the world.

This generation is not marked by events from before 1989, i.e. the times of communist Poland. In
the context of leisure behaviour, Generation Z in Poland is a fully global generation, globalised and
largely culturally homogenised, with similar characteristics in terms of spending free time as in
other countries, in particular those representing the so-called Western culture. Even though
globalisation and cultural homogenisation are often perceived negatively as processes leading to
unsustainable Western-style consumerism or the loss of local identity (see Murray & Overton, 2015;
Ritzer, 2007), in this exceptional case, which is Poland, processes related to adopting free time
behaviour from the West may be considered positively because it means that the flows of global
cultural symbols are no longer limited (as opposed to the period 1945–1989).


**Implications**


The above-mentioned potential negative impact of globalisation and cultural homogenisation may
raise concerns about preserving the local identity of youth in the future. One of the answers to this
problem may lie in the concept of glocalisation, which supports the cultivation of local identity
without excluding the use of global cultural symbols. Thus, there is a need for in-depth research on
how globalisation and cultural homogenisation contribute to changes in leisure behaviour over time
and whether glocalisation can be an answer to preserving local identity for youth. This is particu­
larly true in countries where global cultural symbols have relatively recently begun to reach.

Moreover, it can be assumed that the COVID-19 pandemic is currently contributing to even
greater cultural homogenisation of the leisure behaviour of Generation Z in Poland and that of their
peers in Western countries in the context of using the Internet in their free time. The first analyses
of leisure during a pandemic have already been made (cf. Bond et al., 2020; Cheval et al., 2021),
however, the long-term impact of COVID-19 on free time behaviour remains unknown.

Another interesting research thread is spending free time in virtual space. It seems inevitable to
increase the importance of free time spent on the Internet, social media and widely understood
electronic media. It would be essential to check what relation exists between the real and virtual
space and in which Generation Z is more willing to stay.

In addition, practical implications have been identified. In medium and smaller cities, cultural
education activities (at early stages of education) should be strengthened to increase young people’s
interest in culture. Currently, a large percentage of them do not participate in cultural life under­
stood as going to the cinema, theatre, or museum. In addition to school education, non-formal
activities are required, especially those carried out by public entities, whose actions in the eyes of
young people are often unattractive and misguided. An excellent example in this aspect is Rumia –
a medium-sized Polish city, where a newly built, attractively designed library in a revitalised railroad
station attracts crowds of new readers (Środa-Murawska, 2020). Similar marketing and infrastruc­
tural activities should be carried out in other fields, such as developing young people’s hobbies and
encouraging them to prosocial activities, as all the mentioned activities are significant in positive
youth development (Barber et al., 2009).


LEISURE STUDIES 175


Although global and most popular entertainment in the form of online activities, including social
media, correlates with decreased interest in culture understood as reading books or going to the
theatre, museum, or art gallery, the use of one may not exclude the use of the other. This means that
the marriage of culture with social media and the Internet may remedy the low participation of
youth in culture. Through social media marketing, local cultural institutions should try to promote
their activities to make them attractive in the youngest’s eyes.


**Limitations**


The research results are a particular case - representatives of Generation Z in Poland. Although it
was conducted only in 3 cities, it can be treated as a typical case of young people living in a postsocialist country subject to vital processes of globalisation and cultural homogenisation. In order to
study these processes in-depth in the future, research should be conducted in several countries
according to a single pattern, divided into post-socialist countries, Western European countries, etc.
This study presents a general approach to leisure time and is based on the main forms of spending
it. Therefore, it could not take into account many other factors, such as leisure constraints. These
issues should be expanded in future research.
