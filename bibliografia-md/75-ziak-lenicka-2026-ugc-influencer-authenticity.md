**Abstract:** The growing importance of social media is fundamentally transforming brands’
marketing communication with consumers, with user-generated content (UGC) and influencergenerated content playing a pivotal role in this process. The aim of this paper is to identify
how these two forms of digital communication differ in terms of perceived authenticity and
credibility, and to examine their implications for consumer responses in the online
environment. The study is grounded in the assumption that UGC is typically associated with
higher levels of spontaneity and social proof, whereas influencer-generated content may
enhance reach and professionalise brand presentation, while simultaneously reinforcing
perceptions of commercial intent. Using experimental A/B testing, the findings indicate that
UGC is perceived as more authentic than influencer-generated content. Perceived authenticity
was also found to positively influence brand trust, which in turn significantly predicted purchase
intention. These results highlight authenticity as a key mechanism shaping consumer trust and
behavioural responses in digital marketing communication. The study contributes to the
literature on digital branding and provides practical implications for brands seeking to balance
influencer collaborations with more organic forms of consumer-generated communication.

**Keywords:** authenticity, credibility, consumer trust, user - generated content (UGC),
influencer marketing

**INTRODUCTION**


The rapid diffusion of social media platforms has transformed how brands communicate with
consumers. In this evolving digital ecosystem, two dominant content modalities have emerged
as central to brands’ communication strategies: user-generated content (UGC) and influencergenerated content (often discussed as influencer marketing content). Social media have
become a central environment for information, entertainment, interaction, and marketing,
thereby reshaping how consumers evaluate brands and how brands design communication
strategies. While UGC is commonly framed as spontaneous, peer-driven communication
leveraging social proof and perceived authenticity, influencer-generated content is typically
professionally curated, extending reach and enhancing brand presentation (Kumar, 2024; Kohli
& Gupta, 2024). These contrasting formats directly shape consumers’ perceptions of
authenticity and credibility, which subsequently influence trust formation, engagement, and
purchase-related outcomes (Alamyar, 2025).

A key theoretical and managerial challenge is that UGC and IGC are both frequently framed as
“authentic” alternatives to traditional advertising, yet the two formats differ substantially in
their typical production conditions, perceived commercial intent, and the psychological
mechanisms through which they shape consumer outcomes such as trust, brand perception,
electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), and purchase intention (Usman & Wijaya, 2025; Muda &

that links authenticity and credibility perceptions to consumer responses in digital contexts,
with particular emphasis on how social proof, source credibility, parasocial interaction,

effects of UGC and IGC (Kumagaya et al., 2025).


This paper seeks to identify how user-generated and influencer-generated content differ in
terms of perceived authenticity and credibility, and to examine their implications for consumer
engagement, trust, and purchase intentions. By integrating insights from source credibility
theory and persuasion knowledge theory, the study contributes to the ongoing debate on the
effectiveness of digital brand communication in trust-sensitive and participatory online
markets.


**LITERATURE REVIEW**


**Authenticity, Credibility, and Trust in Digital Brand Communication**


In the contemporary digital landscape, authenticity and credibility have emerged as decisive
factors in determining the success of a brand's communication strategy. Digital transformation
has shifted consumer expectations, placing significant pressure on traditional marketing
models to move beyond mere information provision toward fostering genuine trust.
Authenticity in this context is often linked to the perceived genuineness of the content, with
studies indicating that consumers are significantly more likely to perceive user-generated
content (UGC) as authentic compared to content produced directly by brands. This perceived
authenticity stems from the social proof provided by real consumer experiences, which
effectively reduces perceived risks and strengthens the emotional bond between the brand
and its audience. Simultaneously, credibility is shaped by the trustworthiness and relatability
of the source, whether it be a peer or a digital influencer. However, the rise of commercialized
content, particularly within influencer marketing, introduces challenges; for instance,
sponsorship disclosures can trigger ad skepticism, potentially undermining the very credibility
that these partnerships aim to build. Navigating the interplay between professional brand
presentation and spontaneous, credible consumer voices remains a central challenge for
brands seeking to maintain a reliable digital presence (Younis, 2025).


Authenticity in digital brand communication


Perceived authenticity in digital brand communication refers to the extent to which consumers
evaluate content as genuine, sincere, and reflective of real experiences rather than
strategically constructed persuasion (Morhart et al., 2015). In online environments,
authenticity is not an inherent property of the content itself but a subjective consumer
judgment shaped by contextual cues, source characteristics, and relational proximity between
communicator and audience (Beverland, 2005). Content perceived as spontaneous,
experience-based, and aligned with lived consumer realities is more likely to evoke authenticity
perceptions, which subsequently foster relational trust and emotional connection with the
brand.


Credibility in digital brand communication


Perceived credibility represents consumers’ assessment of the trustworthiness, expertise, and
reliability of the message source and the communicated information. Drawing from source

credibility theory, credibility is generally conceptualized as comprising two primary dimensions:
expertise (the perceived knowledge or competence of the communicator) and trustworthiness
(the perceived honesty and integrity of the communicator). In digital communication,
credibility evaluations are also influenced by perceived commercial intent and transparency of
sponsorship. When audiences infer that content is primarily driven by persuasive motives
rather than informative or experiential value, credibility perceptions may decline (Li et al.,
2020; Muda & Hamzah, 2021).


Authenticity vs credibility in digital brand communication


Authenticity is treated as a perception that content reflects genuine experience, sincere
expression, or “real-life” usage, whereas credibility is more explicitly framed as a judgment
about the believability of the message or the trustworthiness and expertise of the source (Muda
& Hamzah, 2021; Vera, 2024). In UGC contexts, authenticity is often associated with content
arising from voluntary consumer experience and peer communication, which is argued to
render UGC comparatively non-commercial, neutral, empathetic, and thus credible relative to
marketer-produced content. In influencer contexts, perceived authenticity is frequently
discussed as a driver (or antecedent) of influencer source credibility, which in turn supports
brand trust (Lim & Keni, 2025; Havrylenko, 2025).


Both perceived authenticity and perceived credibility function as key antecedents of consumer
responses in digital environments. Research in persuasion and trust formation consistently
demonstrates that content evaluated as authentic and credible increases brand trust, enhances
positive brand attitudes, and strengthens behavioral intentions, including engagement and
purchase intention. Authenticity tends to operate through affective mechanisms by fostering
emotional resonance and identification, whereas credibility often influences cognitive
evaluations related to message reliability and source competence (Audrezet et al., 2020; Lou
& Yuan, 2019). Together, these constructs shape the persuasive effectiveness of digital brand
communication.


Trust in digital brand communication


Multiple studies explicitly position consumer trust as a central mediating mechanism between
digital content attributes (e.g., authenticity, social proof, credibility) and consumer outcomes
(e.g., brand perception, brand image, purchase intention) (Usman & Wijaya, 2025; Kumagaya
et al., 2025; Pramesti & Alversia, 2023). In UGC-focused models, trust is theorized to develop
through perceptions that peers provide relatable, experience-based information and through
social proof signals embedded in review metrics and peer engagement (Usman & Wijaya,
2025; Vera, 2024). In influencer-focused models, trust is constructed through perceived
influencer honesty and trustworthiness, which build credibility and then brand trust in a
stimulus-organism-response (SOR) logic (Havrylenko, 2025).


Trust formation is presented as increasingly fragile in contemporary digital environments, UGCbased trust can be undermined by overclaiming or credibility problems, while influencer-based
trust can erode due to commercialization and saturation. This fragility makes authenticity and
credibility not only desirable attributes but also contested resources in brand communication
(Kumagaya et al., 2025; Xiao-hua et al., 2022).


**User generated content (UGC)**


UGC (user-generated content) is content created by social media users themselves about
brands, products, or services, which is then disseminated on social media platforms (Hussain
et al., 2024) and often includes reviews, ratings, photos, or videos from the user environment
(Joshi, 2025). In the context of online marketing, UGC is traditionally contrasted with companygenerated content (FGC), although both forms can strengthen brand communication, UGC is
often perceived as a more authentic source of information due to its origin and social context

(Zahir, 2025), with multimodal UGC (text, visuals, emotions) potentially enhancing perceived
brand trust and audience engagement (Dewatmoko & Nurmartiani, 2025).


A central theoretical explanation for UGC effectiveness is its embeddedness in social proof
dynamics — signals such as likes, shares, reviews, and testimonials that indicate peer
endorsement. In this framing, social proof strengthens perceived credibility and shapes brand
perception through consumer trust, making trust a mediator between peer signals and brand
evaluation (Usman & Wijaya, 2025). Complementing this view, research on UGC’s role in brand
trust formation emphasizes social proof as a psychological driver across demographics and
argues that subjective norms, information quality, and source credibility are mechanisms by
which positive UGC perceptions convert into brand trust and purchase intentions (Vera, 2024).


**Influencer marketing**


Influencer marketing is a specific format that is typically found neither at the extreme end of
peer communication (such as UGC) nor at the extreme end of purely commercial advertising.
Its credibility is significantly influenced by the extent to which the audience perceives harmony
between the influencer and the brand, how transparent the collaboration is, and whether the
content appears authentic (Lou & Yuan, 2019).


Influencer typologies in digital marketing are most commonly structured (i) by scale
(mega/macro/micro/nano) and (ii) by content form or domain (e.g., blog, vlogging/video-first,
gaming etc.), because these dimensions shape relationship intensity, perceived expertise, and
the visibility of commercial intent (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Kumar, 2024; Zahir, 2025).


The typology of influencers by scale


1) Mega-influencers are the largest-scale influencers, often comparable to celebrities in terms
of reach and visibility, which makes them suitable for broad audience coverage and rapid brand
awareness generation (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Kumar, 2024). Their credibility may benefit from
public visibility and perceived authority, making them useful for large-scale campaigns and
market entry objectives (Havrylenko, 2025). However, frequent brand partnerships and highly
professionalized content can increase perceptions of commercial intent, thereby reducing
sincerity and authenticity, especially among more skeptical audiences (Zahir, 2025; Usman &
Wijaya, 2025). This shift from trust to skepticism is commonly linked to commercialization and
saturation, making transparency, value alignment, and selective partnerships essential for
maintaining credibility at this scale (Muda & Hamzah, 2021; Kumagaya et al., 2025).


2) Macro-influencers combine relatively large reach with greater topic specialization than
mega-influencers and are often seen as balancing broad appeal with niche authority (Kohli &
Gupta, 2024; Kumar, 2024). This can support credibility when audiences perceive them as
knowledgeable, particularly in campaigns requiring visibility beyond micro-communities or in
cross-border e-commerce contexts (Havrylenko, 2025). At the same time, macro-influencers
face similar challenges to mega-influencers, as higher exposure and frequent sponsorship can
increase perceived commercialization and weaken authenticity and trustworthiness (Usman &
Wijaya, 2025; Zahir, 2025). The literature therefore emphasizes transparency, authenticity,
and credibility safeguards, especially given risks such as hidden sponsorship and fake
engagement (Kumagaya et al., 2025). Overall, macro-influencers remain strategically valuable
for reach and authority, but their effectiveness depends on disclosure discipline, brand–
influencer fit, and authentic product experience (Usman & Wijaya, 2025; Muda & Hamzah,
2021).


3) Micro-influencers are typically niche-focused creators who maintain closer and more
interactive relationships with their audiences, which makes them especially effective in building
engagement and trust within specific communities (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Usman & Wijaya,
2025; Havrylenko, 2025). Research associates them with stronger community-based credibility
due to greater social proximity, more frequent interaction, and higher perceived reliability

(Zahir, 2025). Empirical studies further suggest that micro-influencers often outperform larger
influencer categories in trust- and engagement-related outcomes, particularly in youth and ecommerce contexts (Vera, 2024; Lim & Keni, 2025; Usman & Wijaya, 2025). They are also
commonly perceived as more genuine and relatable because their content appears less
commercialized and more grounded in everyday experience (Usman & Wijaya, 2025; Lim &
Keni, 2025; Vera, 2024). However, their credibility still depends on transparency, consistent
engagement, and alignment between influencer identity and brand values (Muda & Hamzah,
2021; Havrylenko, 2025; Zahir, 2025).


4) Nano-influencers are generally described as the smallest influencer category and are
increasingly viewed as high-authenticity communicators whose impact stems from closeness
and relational intimacy rather than broad reach (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Zahir, 2025; Lim & Keni,
2025). Evidence suggests that nano-influencers can be particularly effective in strengthening
trust and emotional connection, including through parasocial relationship mechanisms (Lim &
Keni, 2025). Similarly, research indicates that micro- and nano-influencers can generate strong
trust and engagement because they enable more personalized and authentic interactions that
reduce psychological distance (Vera, 2024). Their effectiveness is often linked to relatability,
perceived reliability, and sustained relevance over time rather than follower counts alone
(Zahir, 2025; Lim & Keni, 2025). At the same time, the literature points to a trade-off: while
nano-influencers may benefit from sincerity and authenticity cues, they may be less effective
in contexts where expertise and authority are more important, highlighting a tension between
intimacy-based authenticity and expertise-based credibility (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Kumagaya
et al., 2025).


In the influencer domain, credibility is repeatedly described as central to whether influencer
marketing translates into brand trust and persuasion. A clear theoretical articulation is provided
through SOR-based modeling: influencer honesty and trustworthiness shape perceived
influencer credibility, which then positively influences brand trust, with credibility mediating
the honesty/trustworthiness to brand trust relationship (Kohli & Gupta, 2024; Havrylenko,
2025).


Scholarship characterizes influencer marketing as shifting from trust to skepticism due to
commercialization, saturation, and heightened consumer awareness, raising concerns about
inauthenticity, brand control, and declining trust. This creates a “credibility dilemma” brands
use influencers partly to obtain an authentic tone, yet the very presence of sponsorship cues
can heighten perceptions of commercial intent and reduce persuasion (Xiao-hua et al., 2022;
Firdaus, 2025).


The influencer literature stresses heterogeneity across influencer types. Conceptual discussion
argues that micro-influencers may build stronger trust due to perceived accessibility, while
macro-influencers can face greater credibility risks due to overexposure and commercialization
(Xiao-hua et al., 2022). Empirical Gen Z evidence is also mixed: one study finds that influencer
credibility does not significantly affect purchase intention in a skincare context, whereas UGC
and brand awareness do (Ali, 2025). By contrast, other work emphasizes that influencer
honesty/trustworthiness influence credibility and brand trust and that authenticity-related
constructs support credibility formation (Lim & Keni, 2025; Havrylenko, 2025).


Taken together, the references imply that influencer effectiveness depends on (i) the specific
outcome being measured (e.g., brand trust vs purchase intention), (ii) influencer type and
perceived commercial intensity, and (iii) whether credibility is successfully constructed through
authenticity cues, ethical transparency, and relational mechanisms such as parasocial
interaction and self-congruity (Lim & Keni, 2025; Havrylenko, 2025; Xiao-hua et al., 2022;
Firdaus, 2025; Ali, 2025).


Despite the growing body of research on user-generated content and influencer-generated
communication, several gaps remain in the existing literature. Prior studies have extensively

content tends to be more strategic and professionally curated, empirical evidence directly
comparing these two formats under controlled conditions remains limited. As a result, there is
still insufficient understanding of whether differences in content origin translate into
differences in perceived authenticity, brand trust, and purchase intention.


**METHODOLOGY**


The theoretical framework was developed through a structured review of contemporary
academic literature in the fields of digital marketing communication, influencer marketing, and
consumer behavior. The literature review focused primarily on peer-reviewed journal articles
indexed in international scientific databases. The literature review allowed the identification of
key constructs and their interrelationships, which subsequently informed the formulation of
research hypotheses and the operationalization of variables in the empirical study.


This study employed a quantitative experimental design to examine differences between usergenerated content (UGC) and influencer-generated content in terms of perceived authenticity,
brand trust, and purchase intention. A between-subjects A/B testing approach was
implemented. Each participant was exposed to a single simulated Instagram post promoting
the same product. The visual format, product description, and core message were held
constant across conditions. The only manipulated element was the source of the content (peer
user vs. influencer profile). This approach ensured internal validity by isolating the effect of
content origin while controlling for message-related factors.


Data were collected through an online questionnaire administered between January 5 and
February 5, 2026. The survey link was distributed electronically among students of the
Bratislava University of Economics and Business (EUBA). Participation was voluntary and
anonymous. A total of 212 respondents completed the questionnaire. All responses were
screened for completeness, and no cases were excluded from the final analysis.


Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions:


UGC condition (n = 106)
Influencer condition (n = 106)
The sampling approach can be described as convenience-based with random assignment to
experimental conditions. The respondents consisted primarily of active social media users aged
18–35 years, reflecting a demographic segment frequently exposed to digital brand
communication and influencer marketing practices. This population is considered particularly
relevant for research on authenticity perceptions and online persuasion mechanisms.


Based on prior research in digital marketing communication and source credibility theory, the
following hypotheses were formulated:

  H1: User-generated content will be perceived as more authentic than influencer

  H2: User-generated content will generate higher levels of brand trust compared to

This section presents the statistical analyses conducted to test the proposed hypotheses. The
analytical procedure followed a structured approach. First, the internal consistency of the
multi-item scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients. Second, descriptive
statistics (means and standard deviations) were calculated for all key variables. Third,
independent samples t-tests were employed to examine mean differences between the UGC
and influencer conditions. Finally, simple linear regression analyses were conducted to assess
the predictive relationships among perceived authenticity, brand trust, and purchase intention.


All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. A significance threshold of p
< 0.05 was applied throughout the analyses.

**CONCLUSION**


This study investigated the differences between user-generated content (UGC) and influencergenerated content in digital brand communication, with a particular emphasis on perceived
authenticity, brand trust, and purchase intention. Using an experimental A/B testing, the
findings demonstrate that content origin significantly influences consumer perceptions and
subsequent behavioral responses. The results show that UGC is perceived as more authentic
than influencer-generated content. Furthermore, perceived authenticity was found to positively
influence brand trust, which in turn significantly predicted purchase intention. These findings
confirm the central role of authenticity in shaping trust formation processes in digital
environments and support the assumption that trust acts as a key mechanism linking content
perception to consumer behavior.


From a theoretical perspective, the study contributes to the literature by empirically validating
the relationship between authenticity, trust, and behavioral intention in the context of digital
marketing communication. It further suggests that although influencer marketing remains an
important strategic tool, organically generated content may generate stronger authenticity
cues among younger digital audiences.


From a managerial perspective, the findings imply that brands should not rely exclusively on
influencer collaborations but should actively encourage and integrate user-generated content
to strengthen perceived authenticity and consumer trust. Future research should examine
more diverse populations, additional product categories, and real-life behavioral data to further
validate the proposed relationships.


Future research should test the model across broader consumer groups, product categories,
and real-world social media settings to strengthen generalizability.
